1.1 Interactive Narratives
”Imagine that Harry Potter can decide not to go to Hogwarts. Or Frodo decides to keep the
ring and become the best thief in the Middle Earth.”[1]
Interactive narratives are also known as choose-your-own-adventure stories, as the di-
rection of the plot is decided by the user. [2] Interactive digital narratives (IDN) tightly
integrate interactivity and narrative.[3]
1.1.1 Branching narratives
Branching narrative structures are a specific kind of interactive narrative. In role-playing
games, the story is built around fixed decision points, also called nodes. When a player
reaches such a node, their choice determines which pre-written story branch they experi-
ence next. Even though branching narratives can offer many possible paths, each individ-
ual playthrough is linear in hindsight, one story among many possibilities. Branching can
therefore be seen as the mechanism that enables immersion into a story which, from the
inside, feels personal.[4]
1.1.2 User engagement in interactive narratives
Transportation
Transportation refers to the cognitive and emotional immersion a person experiences when
absorbed in a story . During transportation, one’s attention, emotions, and mental imagery
become so focused on the narrative world that it often causes the real world to fade from
awareness. Transportation can be strengthened by interactivity as the reader or player
“enacts rather than witnesses the story ,” which can make the experience more immersive
and enjoyable. Transportability is an individual’s general tendency to become absorbed in
stories; those high in transportability are more easily immersed. [2]
User Control
User engagement in an interactive narrative is closely tied to the user’s sense of control.
When a user is given control inside the interactive narrative we often refer to it as agency .
Effectance is the subjective experience of agency which gives the player a sense of com-
petence and influence that produces a feeling of being “in control.” For this feeling to
emerge, players must see evidence that their actions actually affect the world. If they do
not, engagement drops significantly .[4]

Identification
Identification is the process of merging with a character, adopting their goals, perspective,
and emotions. It involves stepping into the character’s shoes and feeling empathy for
their situation. However, in interactive narratives, the ability to influence story events
allows users to act as the character or as themselves, and they often choose the latter.[2]
Given moral agency in an RPG with a branching structure, it was found that users made
decisions as they would in real life, had high empathy for non-player characters (NPCs),
and experienced emotions like those in actual interpersonal interactions.[4] Because users
actively determine the character’s actions, they feel a sense of responsibility for the story’s
outcomes.[2]
Narrative Structure
Branching complexity refers to the number of different possible paths that are available in
the system/story graph. Players build mental models of how the game reacts to their ac-
tions, for example: ‘If I choose to save this character, maybe they’ll help me later.’ This is a
mental model of causal agency . Such models are closely aligned with players’ mental mod-
els of the overall narrative structure, meaning that when players imagine how their choices
matter, they are really imagining the shape of the story’s branching paths. Together, these
mental models shape the perceived complexity of the narrative, which in turn influences
experienced richness. Greater perceived complexity produces greater richness, which sup-
ports engagement by making the game feel more meaningful and capable of developing
the player’s skills.[4]
1.1.3 Designing Interactive Stories
Designing interactive stories poses a challenge, as the increased user control can lead to a
decrease in narrative structure.[2]
Spierling[5] proposes a framework that integrates interaction design principles as nar-
rative techniques for interactive digital storytelling (IDN). Drawing on Norman’s[6] con-
cepts of affordances, feedback, mapping, and constraints, and Foley et al.’s[7] four levels
of interaction design, Spierling reinterprets these as layers of narrative construction. At
the conceptual level, authors define the overarching story logic and player role; at the
semantic level, they design narrative functions of actions and their consequences; at the
syntactic level, they determine patterns and sequences for how events and feedback are
presented; and at the lexical level, they define concrete input and output mechanisms.
This hierarchy allows story and interaction to be designed as one unified system.

1.2.1 Eye Tracking and Gaze Interaction
[8], [9]
Eye tracking is a technology that records where and how long a person looks at visual
stimuli, capturing gaze direction, fixation duration, and saccadic movements. As reviewed
by Nov´ak et al. (2024), eye tracking has evolved from a niche experimental tool into a
central component of usability and user experience (UX) research. It allows for detailed,
quantitative insights into how users visually interact with interfaces or environments, com-
plementing traditional usability measures such as task completion time or subjective sat-
isfaction. Modern eye tracking technologies, including infrared-based systems (e.g., Tobii
devices) and budget webcam-based trackers, can identify fixations and saccades to deter-
mine Areas of Interest (AOIs) and patterns of visual attention, which can then be mapped
to interface elements or physical products.
At the core of gaze-based research are two primary eye movement types: fixations,
where the eye remains relatively stable allowing information processing, and saccades,
the rapid movements between fixations that enable visual scanning. Understanding these
movements provides valuable information about user attention, cognitive load, and task
intent (Rayner, 1978; Hafed, 2011). Nov´ak et al. emphasize that while eye tracking offers
powerful implicit data, it must be triangulated with interaction data (e.g., clicks, scrolling)
or biometric measures to contextualize user behavior and emotion.
Building on these foundations, gaze interaction expands the use of eye tracking from
passive observation to active control. As demonstrated in Sibert and Jacob’s seminal study
Evaluation of Eye Gaze Interaction, gaze can function as a natural, efficient input method.
Their experiments compared gaze-based selection using dwell time to conventional mouse
interaction. Results showed that gaze selection was faster than mouse-based selection,
leveraging the inherent quickness of eye movements. The technique, grounded in the
physiology of fixations and saccades, translated natural eye behavior into interaction com-
mands by recognizing stable gaze points (approximately 100–150 ms dwell) as intentional
selections. This made interaction feel intuitive, almost anticipatory , freeing users’ hands
for other tasks—an advantage for large-screen environments and accessibility applications.
From a systems perspective, Sibert and Jacob’s architecture integrated real-time eye
tracking data with a User Interface Management System that processed fixations and
mapped gaze points to nearby on-screen objects. Their algorithm considered spatial and
temporal thresholds to distinguish meaningful fixations from natural eye jitter, enabling
robust, real-time interaction despite technological constraints of early eye trackers. This
early work laid the foundation for modern gaze-based systems in virtual and augmented

reality , automotive interfaces, and assistive technologies.
Contemporary research, as summarized by Nov´ak et al., extends these early principles
by combining gaze data with machine learning techniques for automated pattern recog-
nition, emotion detection, and adaptive interface behavior. For instance, gaze data can
now inform UX optimization by identifying frustration patterns or predicting user inten-
tions in real time. Machine learning models enhance event detection, classify fixations
and saccades, and map gaze to interface elements automatically , reducing manual data
interpretation.
Overall, the convergence of gaze interaction and eye tracking represents a shift from
passive observation of visual behavior to active, intelligent systems that adapt to users’
attention and intent. Gaze-based input holds particular promise for hands-free control, ac-
cessibility , and immersive environments, offering a natural, low-effort interaction paradigm
that complements other modalities such as speech and gesture.
1.2.2 Mouse vs. Eye-Tracking
[10]
When comparing mouse interaction with eye-tracking, each input method offers clear
strengths and limitations that make them suitable for different contexts. The mouse re-
mains the most accurate tool for object selection. In clustered or occluded environments,
users were able to place the cursor with very high control, often without the need to
zoom in. Its familiarity and reliability also play an important role: participants in stud-
ies had decades of experience with mouse use, which meant that little to no training was
required. Moreover, performance with a mouse is consistent and not affected by factors
such as lighting, user physiology , or fatigue, issues that often impact the accuracy of eye-
tracking systems. Despite these advantages, the mouse shows some weaknesses. In simple
scenarios with clearly separated objects, eye-tracking with a confirmation click proved to
be faster on average. The physical effort of using a mouse also makes it less accessible for
motor-impaired users. In addition, ingrained habits such as double-clicking occasionally
led to unintended errors.
By contrast, eye-tracking interaction leverages the fact that eye movements precede
hand actions, allowing users to select targets almost as soon as they look at them. In non-
clustered environments, eye-tracking with a confirmation click was not only the fastest
method but also produced the lowest average error rates and the lowest reported cognitive
load. It provides hands-free accessibility , which is especially valuable for users with mo-
tor impairments, and it can enhance immersion in interactive experiences such as games
by creating a more natural, direct mode of input. However, eye-tracking is limited by
precision. Natural eye jitter (micro-saccades) and calibration issues reduce its accuracy
compared to mouse control, often forcing users to zoom or accept higher error rates in

more complex tasks. The “Midas Touch” problem—where not everything the eye looks at
should be selected—remains a central challenge, usually mitigated by dwell-time or con-
firmation mechanisms that add delay . Furthermore, accuracy varied significantly between
users and across different regions of the screen, being influenced by factors such as light-
ing conditions, eyewear, and even eye color. In clustered environments, these weaknesses
led to longer task completion times and higher cognitive load than mouse interaction.
Choosing between mouse and eye-tracking therefore depends on the intended task,
user context, and system goals. For simple and non-clustered tasks, eye-tracking with con-
firmation click can outperform the mouse, while for complex, clustered environments, the
mouse remains superior in both speed and cognitive load. User accessibility is another
important factor: for motor-impaired users, eye-tracking may be the only viable option,
whereas for typical users, mouse input remains the safest and most efficient choice. The
goals of the system also matter. If immersion, natural interaction, or hands-free operation
are priorities, eye-tracking is attractive. If precision, reliability , and low error rates are crit-
ical, the mouse is preferable. In many cases, hybrid solutions offer the best compromise,
for example by using gaze to indicate a target and mouse, keyboard, or voice as a confir-
mation method, thereby balancing speed and accuracy while minimizing the weaknesses
of each approach.
1.2.3 Voice User Interfaces
[11]
Voice User Interfaces (VUIs) provide a natural and intuitive way for users to inter-
act with digital systems by speaking rather than relying on manual input. This modality
leverages speech as humans’ primary communication channel, making interaction with
technology more conversational and accessible.
One of the major strengths of VUIs is their hands-free and eyes-free nature, which al-
lows interaction while the user is engaged in other tasks. This makes them particularly
suitable for mobile contexts, accessibility applications, or scenarios where visual attention
must remain on the environment. VUIs also provide low entry barriers; users do not re-
quire extensive training, as speaking is already a highly familiar skill. Studies highlight
their potential to increase accessibility for visually impaired users and those with motor
disabilities, as well as their role in improving inclusivity in interaction design. Additionally ,
VUIs can enhance immersion and naturalness in narrative-driven or game environments,
as they allow players to interact in ways closer to real-world dialogue.
Despite these advantages, VUIs also present significant challenges. Speech recognition
accuracy is a persistent issue, with performance affected by background noise, accents,
speech impairments, and language variety . Errors in recognition can quickly lead to frus-
tration, particularly in high-stakes or time-sensitive tasks. VUIs are also context-sensitive;
they often perform well in quiet, private spaces but become less practical in noisy or pub-
6

lic environments where speaking aloud may be socially awkward or disruptive. Another
weakness is the limited discoverability of commands. Unlike graphical interfaces where
options are visible, VUIs often rely on users knowing or guessing the correct commands,
which can increase cognitive load and learning effort. Finally , there are privacy and trust
concerns, as VUIs typically require open microphones and sometimes cloud processing,
raising user worries about surveillance and data security .
The decision to implement voice interaction should be based on the intended context
and system goals. VUIs are particularly valuable when hands-free operation is necessary ,
such as in driving, industrial work, or accessibility-focused applications. They are also at-
tractive in immersive or narrative scenarios, where conversational input enhances realism
and user engagement. However, VUIs are less suitable in noisy , public, or socially sensi-
tive environments, and they should not be the sole input method for tasks that require
high precision, reliability , or speed, given the potential for recognition errors. A common
recommendation is to integrate VUIs as part of a multimodal system, where speech com-
plements but does not fully replace other input methods such as touch, mouse, or gaze.
This hybrid approach mitigates weaknesses while still leveraging the strengths of natural
voice interaction.
1.2.4 The Midas Touch Problem and Advances in Reliable Eye-Tracking
Interaction
[12] , [13]
A persistent challenge in gaze-based interaction systems is the Midas touch problem, a
phenomenon where the system cannot distinguish between a user’s natural gaze behavior
and intentional selection commands. In other words, when users simply look around
to explore a scene, the system might misinterpret these fixations as activation signals,
triggering unintended commands (Jacob, 1990). This issue arises from the dual role of
the human eye—as both a perceptual organ and a potential control device—making it
difficult for gaze interfaces to reliably separate seeing from doing.
Over the years, several approaches have been proposed to mitigate the Midas touch
problem, aiming to make eye-based interfaces more robust and intuitive.
1. Dwell-Time Processing
The most common solution has been dwell-time selection, where a command is trig-
gered only when the gaze remains fixed on a target for a specified period (typically be-
tween 300–1100 ms) ( ˇSpakov and Miniotas, 2004). The assumption is that intentional
fixations are longer than incidental glances. Studies have shown that dwell-time can be
efficient—even outperforming mouse clicks in certain controlled tasks (Sibert and Jacob,
2000). However, this method introduces a delicate trade-off: short dwell times increase
false activations, while long ones slow down interaction and cause visual fatigue (Verte-
gaal, 2008; Pfeuffer et al., 2020).
7
PAGE 11
Hyperobjects in VR Chapter 1
To refine this, Velichkovsky et al. (2014) proposed a focal fixation extraction approach
that identifies deliberate visual commands based on the properties of fixation duration
and context. By empirically setting the activation threshold to the mean fixation duration
(around 325 ms), the method differentiated between task-related fixations and ordinary
eye movements, providing a data-driven way to reduce false positives. This approach
moved toward a more physiological, adaptive model of gaze intention rather than fixed
time thresholds.
2. Smooth Pursuits and Gaze Gestures
Alternative paradigms include smooth pursuit-based methods, where users follow a
moving stimulus with their eyes to activate a command (Vidal et al., 2013; Velloso et al.,
2016). This makes it easier to confirm intentional engagement since following a trajec-
tory requires active participation. Similarly , gaze gestures—predefined sequences of eye
movements (e.g., drawing a shape or direction pattern)—have been developed to signal
intent explicitly (Drewes and Schmidt, 2007). These methods successfully reduce acciden-
tal activations but may lead to visual fatigue and slower performance during extended use,
especially for novice users.
3. Multimodal Interaction
A more recent trend involves multimodal approaches, which combine gaze with sec-
ondary input modalities (e.g., touch, speech, sound, or head movement) to separate point-
ing and selection actions. This separation significantly enhances reliability and reduces
unintentional triggers.
One prominent example is the EyeTAP system (Parisay et al., 2021), which integrates
eye tracking for pointing with acoustic input (such as a tongue click or microphone tap) for
selection. By requiring a deliberate auditory signal to confirm gaze-based pointing, Eye-
TAP effectively eliminates accidental activations while maintaining hands-free operation.
The technique proved especially useful in accessibility contexts and environments requir-
ing contact-free interaction. Compared with traditional dwell-time and voice-recognition
methods, EyeTAP achieved lower error rates and reduced cognitive workload, demonstrat-
ing that multimodal fusion can make gaze interfaces both efficient and reliable.
4. Toward Adaptive and Intelligent Solutions
Recent work continues to explore adaptive thresholds and contextual recognition—leveraging
machine learning to dynamically interpret gaze patterns, fixation stability , and environ-
mental factors. These models aim to personalize activation criteria based on user behavior,
thereby minimizing false positives while preserving speed and comfort.
1.3 What is tension and how is it created?
[14] Toward a general psychological model of tension and suspense Tension and suspense
are affective states found in everyday life, art, and entertainment. They arise from conflict,
8
PAGE 12
Hyperobjects in VR Chapter 1
dissonance, instability , or uncertainty , and involve future-oriented processes of expecta-
tion, anticipation, and prediction. Initiating events associated with conflict or uncertainty
trigger predictions about possible outcomes; the greater the divergence in their emotional
value, the stronger the experienced tension. Tension creates a desire for resolution and
can be linked to both positive (hope, excitement) and negative (fear, anxiety) emotions,
depending on the outcome expected.
Tension is shaped by several factors: the emotional significance of possible outcomes,
the degree of uncertainty , and the lack of control over events. Importantly , tension unfolds
over time and can occur at both micro- and macro-levels, from the phrasing of a sentence
to the structure of an entire narrative.
Despite its often negative connotations, tension can be pleasurable, especially in aes-
thetic contexts such as music, film, or literature, where it is safe and resolved within fic-
tional frameworks. This makes tension a powerful driver of engagement and immersion.
1.3.1 The General Psychological Model of Tension and Suspense
These affective states are rooted in universal cognitive and emotional mechanisms that
operate across a variety of domains, including music, film, literature, and everyday life, as
per the General Psychological Model of Tension and Suspense [14]. In order to emphasize
that the same basic mechanisms underlie the experience of tension regardless of its con-
text, this model takes a domain-independent stance. Essentially , tension and suspense are
affective states that are distinguished by a yearning for resolution, emotional concern for
potential outcomes, and reliance on predictive, future-oriented mental processes. These
states are characterized by conflict, instability , or uncertainty .
1.3.2 Affective Intensity and Anticipated Outcomes
The subjective intensity of tension is the result of the discrepancy between the affective
values (desirability) of the anticipated outcomes of an event. Tension increases with the
size of the range between extremely positive and extremely negative possible outcomes
(see figure 1.1). As a result, circumstances that have both desirable and undesirable po-
tential outcomes cause tension because they arouse the prospect emotions of hope for
favorable outcomes and fear or anxiety about unfavorable ones. Therefore, tension acts
as a diffuse affective prelude to more distinct emotional responses that follow resolution,
like happiness, relief, or disappointment.
1.3.3 Tension and the Drive for Psychological Homeostasis
At its core, tension is indicative of the human impulse to achieve psychological homeostasis
-the desire to reconcile dissonance and reestablish equilibrium. This concept is consistent
with Festinger’s cognitive dissonance theory , which posits that psychological inconsistency
induces discomfort and leads to resolution [15]. In aesthetic settings like music, dissonant
chords or harmonic deviations from a tonal center create instability and tension that goes
away when stable, consonant harmonies are restored. The affective force of tension is

1.3.4 The Role of Uncertainty and the Paradox of Suspense
Uncertainty is a critical factor in the development of tension; however, its significance is
complicated by the paradox of suspense, which occurs when individuals continue to feel
tension despite the fact that the outcome of an event is already known, as for example,
during repeated exposure to a well-known story or musical composition [14].
This paradox may be attributed to the experience of feigned uncertainty in fictional
contexts, where audiences voluntarily suspend their knowledge of outcomes, and auto-
matic pre-attentive processing in music perception, where tension responses are triggered
by violations of expectation despite familiarity . This paradox shows that although tension
is often increased by uncertainty , uncertainty by itself cannot adequately account for its
persistence.
1.3.5 Temporal Dynamics of Tension
Tension is a temporally dynamic phenomenon that is constantly changing over a range
of time scales. It may manifest at either micro-levels (e.g., within a phrase, sentence, or
musical motif) or macro-levels (e.g., throughout an entire narrative or composition). The
emotional trajectory of tension is influenced by the temporal distance between the ini-
tiating event and its eventual resolution. Conversely , the affective experience may be
weakened by rapid resolution, while it is intensified and sustained by a prolonged resolu-
tion. Because of these time-dependent features, tension is a useful concept for studying
how emotions predict and prepare people. By demonstrating the long-term interactions
between cognitive prediction and emotional experience, tension research does, in fact, pro-

1.3.6 The Pleasure of Tension in Aesthetic Contexts
In aesthetic and entertainment contexts, tension can be a source of pleasure, even though
it frequently manifests as discomfort or anxiety in daily life. This is because the risks as-
sociated with tension are safely contained. Tension creates intense emotions that can be
enjoyed on its own, leading to excitement, thrill, and engagement in these fictional or
artistic settings. Emotional satisfaction and closure are facilitated by the eventual resolu-
tion of tension, which reinforces the rewarding nature of the experience and immersion.
In this way , tension is not only a sign of psychological conflict, but it is also a key part of
enjoying art, getting emotionally involved, and getting lost in a story .

1.4.3 Electroencephalogram (EEG)
Electroencephalography (EEG) uses electrodes on the scalp to record the electrical activity
of the brain. Due to its high temporal resolution, EEG enables researchers to monitor quick
shifts in the neural activity linked to tension and suspense [18] [17].
EEG Frequency Bands and Emotional Arousal
EEG signals are typically divided into frequency bands, each of which is linked to unique
cognitive and emotional states:
•Delta (0.5–4 Hz):Mainly present during deep sleep; not often connected to active
suspense but may show general cortical downregulation.
•Theta (4–8 Hz):Related to emotional processing, memory encoding, and anticipa-
tion. When the viewer is emotionally engaged and anticipating a narrative outcome,
theta power frequently increases.
•Alpha (8–12 Hz):Usually connected to a calm, awake states. Alpha suppression, or
a reduction in alpha power, can be a sign of increased attention and arousal during
a calm, awake state.
•Beta (13–30 Hz):Related to anxiety , alertness, and active cognition. When partici-
pants feel tension or uncertainty , beta power tends to rise.
•Gamma (>30Hz):Correlated with high-level cognitive processes and perceptual
binding. May momentarily rise during intense tension.
Event-Related Potentials (ERPs)
EEG can also captureevent-related potentials (ERPs), which are time-locked neural re-
sponses to discrete stimuli:
•P300:A positive deflection occurring roughly 300 ms after a surprising or attention-
grabbing event. Larger P300 amplitudes reflect higher attentional engagement, often
observed during unexpected plot twists [18].
•Late Positive Potential (LPP):Sustained positive deflection associated with emo-
tional arousal, including suspense and tension. The LPP can reflect the cumulative
impact of prolonged suspenseful stimuli [18].
EEG and Sympathetic–Parasympathetic Interactions
The central-peripheral coupling of suspense, peripheral physiological measures (HR, HRV,
EDA) can be integrated with neural patterns [18]:
• Synchronous neural and autonomic arousal is suggested by the fact that increases in
beta and theta activity frequently occur in tandem with HR and SCR peaks.
• Complementing tonic SCL increases that represent ambient tension, alpha suppres-
sion may indicate cognitive engagement with the unfolding narrative.

1.5 Physiological stress detection
Physiological stress detection commonly uses electrodermal activity (EDA) and blood vol-
ume pulse (BVP), as both signals reflect autonomic responses linked to psychological stress.
One reason for selecting these sensors in the present project is that they are non-intrusive,
easy to attach, and can be comfortably worn on the wrist or hand. Unlike sensors such as
ECG chest straps or respiration belts, EDA and BVP do not interfere with movement and
are suitable for continuous monitoring in interactive applications.
The WESAD dataset [26] is a standard benchmark for wearable stress detection and
provides wrist-based EDA and BVP recorded during baseline, stress, and amusement condi-
tions. Because the dataset uses the same sensing modalities as this project, it is frequently
referenced when evaluating stress-recognition models.
Ninh et al. [27] trained several machine-learning models on the WESAD dataset using
features derived from EDA, BVP, and temperature. Their best-performing approach was
a subject-independent Random Forest classifier trained on baseline-normalized features,
which significantly improved generalization to new participants. The study demonstrates
that EDA and BVP, when combined with an appropriate normalization strategy , are suffi-
cient for effective stress detection using consumer-grade wearable devices.
Although EDA alone is known to be a reliable indicator of physiological arousal [28],
one goal of the present project was to incorporate a machine-learning component, as re-
quired by the project specification. For this reason, a multi-sensor model was explored to
investigate whether combining EDA and BVP-derived metrics (such as heart rate and HRV)
could improve predictive performance over single-signal baselines.
This work follows the same principles observed in prior research by using EDA and
BVP features together with a baseline calibration phase before performing real-time stress
classification.
1.6 Phenomenological Insights
Phenomenology provides a critical framework for comprehending the human experience
in relation to technology , particularly through the works of Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty .
Heidegger’s conception of being is always positioned as being-in-the-world (In-der-Welt-
sein), a way of living that emphasizes active participation over objective observation. This
perspective emphasizes the relational and situated nature of experience, in which tools
and technologies are not neutral intermediaries but rather contribute to the evolution of
the structure of being. With his focus on embodiment, Merleau-Ponty expands on this line
of thinking. Perception is an enactment of the body as a lived and intentional structure
rather than an abstract cognitive function [29]. Instead of being an object in space, the
body is what makes space meaningful and inhabitable.
Phenomenological analysis establishes a basis for comprehending the interaction and
immersion in technological environments as inherently corporeal and situated experiences.
Current studies on digital and virtual media highlight that the bodily integration of percep-

1.7.2 Different interrogation approaches
J. Eastwood[33] Diagnosticity relates to the ability of an interrogation approach to get
confessions from the guilty while avoiding false confessions from the innocent. In this
article, Eastwood compares the relative diagnosticity of a persuasion-based and a dialogue-
based interrogation approach.
In persuasion-based tactics, such as the Reid technique, the interrogator stresses their
certainty of the suspect’s guilt from the start (e.g., “There is no doubt you took the money”;
direct confrontation) and offers face-saving justifications to minimize or normalize the
crime (e.g., “Maybe you took the money to buy food for your family”; theme development).
Research suggests that these kinds of tactics are effective in generating confessions from
guilty suspects, but may cause innocent suspects to confess as well.
In Dialogue-based approaches, such as the PEACE model, present case evidence in a
non-accusatorial fashion to give the suspect a chance to clarify (e.g., “You mentioned you
were not present at the bar. However, we have two eyewitnesses who claim to have seen
you there. How can you account for this?).
In this study , they followed the Russano cheating paradigm, where participants are ei-
ther induced or not induced to cheat on an experimental task to create innocent and guilty
conditions. Some interesting findings were that in the persuasion-based approach, most
participants confessed during the first attempt, while for the dialogue-based approach, the
17
PAGE 21
Hyperobjects in VR Chapter 1
largest proportion of confessions were made during the third and final attempt, meaning
that guilty participants in the dialogue-based condition did not confess until the strongest
evidence was presented.
C. Meissner[34]
The article distinguishes accusatorial vs information-gathering interrogation styles: the
former emphasizes psychological control, confrontation, presumption of guilt; the latter
emphasizes rapport, cooperation, and eliciting the suspect’s narrative.
Accusatorial methods often employ maximization (threats, exaggeration) and mini-
mization (offering face-saving rationales), plus false-evidence ploys, to pressure a confes-
sion. Such methods risk false confessions, particularly from vulnerable individuals (youth,
mentally ill, suggestible) or under conditions of stress, fatigue, or perceived coercion.
In contrast, information-gathering approaches begin by building rapport, encouraging
the suspect to provide their own account, and strategically introducing evidence later to
resolve inconsistencies. The benefit of information-gathering is diagnostic value: it tends
to preserve or improve true confession rates while reducing false confessions (i.e. greater
accuracy) in experimental settings.
The authors advocate further research to refine best practices, validate techniques
across contexts, and understand moderating factors (e.g. cultural, situational) that in-
fluence which style is optimal.

1.10 Affect Adaptive Games
Affective computing has been a prominent topic within human–computer interaction (HCI)
research. By taking the current affective state of the user into account, it has the potential
to provide optimal emotional experiences.
However, in a systematic review by Croissant et al.[45], they found that there is a lack
of sufficient systematic evidence to determine whether affective adaptation in video games
successfully enhances their positive effects on players. One contributing issue they identify

is that many studies focus primarily on exploration of technological solutions regarding
emotion recognition and adaptation, and not in the evaluation of affect-adaptive games.
Affective design
Affective design focuses on three core components:[45]
• Emotion sensing.
• Computational emotion modeling.
• Adaptation.
These components can be addressed through the Affective Feedback Loop. This loop
describes the emotional interaction between player and game. It consists of three phases:
• Emotion Elicitation: The game presents various elements such as NPC behaviors,
environments, narrative events ect. that trigger emotional responses in the player.
• Emotion Detection/Modelling: the game detects the emotional reactions of the player,
and interprets those reactions according to the context of the game.
• Emotion Adaptation and Expression: based on that interpretation, the game changes
its behavior, content, or NPC expressions to respond appropriately to the player. This,
in turn, affects the player, making them respond through game actions and emotional
reactions, which leads us to step 1 again.

2.5 Creating the Interrogation System
Our goal is to design an interrogation system in which the interrogator would react dy-
namically to both the user’s chosen dialogue options and their physiological state (whether
they are stressed or non-stressed). This is important because we want the interaction to
feel, as if the interrogator were reading not only the user’s words, but also their body
language. Therefore we treated the physiological state as a nonverbal layer of communi-
cation. Much like real body language that can reveal tension or dishonesty even when a
person’s verbal response says otherwise, for example, looking away while lying.
In our system, each dialogue choice and physiological reading forms a pairing that
determines the tone and style of the interrogator’s response. While the core meaning of
the reply remains the same, its word choice and tone shifts. To ensure these reactions felt
natural, we mapped the relationships between user states and interrogator responses, as
shown in Figure 2.9.

3.1 System Implementation in Unreal Engine
The System is implemented in Unreal Engine 5.6 and consists of several modules that
handle asset management, environment setup, character creation, animation, audio, and
real time physiological input. These modules work together to present the narrative and
adapt the experience according to the user state. Bellow is an in depth description of how
each of these systems were implemented.
3.1.1 File Manager
A Blueprint in Unreal Engine receives the predicted user state through a Lab Streaming
Layer inlet. The inlet reads the real time classification output from the Python system and
provides a string value corresponding to baseline, relax, or stress. The Blueprint contains
a simple state handler that increments separate counters each time one of these states
is received. These counters allow the system to track how often the player enters each
physiological state during the experience. (See Figure 3.1)
For evaluation and documentation, the Blueprint also logs these incoming states to a
local file. The file is written incrementally during runtime, enabling later analysis of user
behaviour, model consistency , and the relation between in-game events and physiological
responses. This log file serves as the primary record for validating the real time inference
pipeline and its influence on the interactive experience.

offer a quick, reliable method for producing dialogue and character voices. This method

model and subsequent post-processing to standardize vocal timbre and establish consistent
character-specific identities across the sessions. Even though the method worked well
overall, there were sporadic phonetic substitutions where some words were represented as

3.2 Machine Learning Model for Stress Detection
The machine learning component is based on supervised classification using features ex-
tracted from EDA and BVP signals in the WESAD dataset. After preprocessing, the signals
are segmented into five second windows. For each window, the system computes mean
heart rate, RMSSD heart rate variability , EDA mean, EDA standard deviation, and the stan-
dard deviation of the first derivative of EDA. These features capture changes in autonomic
activity that correspond to baseline, relax, and stress conditions.

To account for subject specific differences in physiology , baseline segments in WESAD
are used to compute per subject reference values. These values are subtracted from the raw
features to create baseline normalized features. The model is trained on these normalized
features, as this improves the ability to generalize across subjects.

PAGE 52

A Random Forest classifier is selected for its robustness to noise and its ability to model
nonlinear relations in the feature space. GroupKFold cross validation is applied with each
subject treated as one group. This ensures that no data from the same subject appear in
both the training and test sets. The model achieves a subject independent accuracy of
approximately sixty five percent, which is consistent with results reported for wrist based
WESAD signals.
After training, the model is exported as a joblib file for integration into the real time
system. During deployment, incoming features are normalized using the user baseline
collected at start up, and the trained classifier predicts one of three states. The output is
used to drive the adaptive behaviour in the game, and is streamed via Lab Streaming Layer
(See section 3.2.1).
3.2.1 Lab Streaming Layer
Lab Streaming Layer (LSL) [67] is an open-source middleware framework for real-time
streaming, synchronization, and recording of time-series data across devices and applica-
tions, such as eye tracking, motion capture, EEG, game events, etc.
The Lab Streaming Layer (LSL) was employed as the primary middleware for the real-
time acquisition, synchronization, and distribution of physiological data between the mea-
surement system, analysis scripts, and the game engine. The raw sensor data was uni-
formly timestamped and device-independently accessible through the use of the OpenSignals
software to record EDA and BVP signals and expose them as LSL streams. A Python script
received these streams via LSL inlets, executed fundamental preprocessing and feature
extraction, and transmitted compact representations of the physiological state to an Un-
real Engine 5.6 blueprint inlet. These input values were translated into logic and game
parameters in Unreal, allowing the interactive experience to instantly adjust to the user’s
physiological state.

4.2 Results from Paper
4.2.1 Semi-Structured Interviews
Following the playtesting session, participants completed a short, semi-structured inter-
view to capture qualitative impressions of the experience. Overall, the interviews indicate
a strongly positive reception, with many participants describing the experience as engag-
ing, immersive, and technically impressive. The interrogation setting, lighting, graphics,
and voice acting were frequently highlighted as effective in supporting narrative tension
and presence.
Several recurring improvement areas emerged. Multiple participants reported initial
confusion regarding the narrative context and flashback structure, suggesting that a clearer
introduction and a more elaborated ending could improve narrative clarity and closure.
Additionally , while dialogue choices were generally well received, participants often noted
that options felt limited or too similar, occasionally reducing the sense of agency and
control.
Finally , participants pointed to technical and audiovisual issues such as low audio lev-
els, unnatural animations, and lack of eye contact as immersion-breaking. Some also
expressed a desire for clearer feedback on how their physiological state influenced the
interrogation, indicating that the adaptive elements were not always perceptible.

4.2.6 Adaptivity
Linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between adapt-
ability and self-reported experience measures. Immersion scores significantly predicted
adaptability ,b= 0.50,t(25) = 2.36,p=.026, with higher immersion associated with
higher usability ratings, accounting for 18% of the variance (R 2 =.182). Similarly , con-
trol ratings also significantly predicted adaptability ,b= 0.23,t(25) = 2.26,p=.033,
explaining 17% of the variance (R 2 =.170). Valence emerged as the strongest predictor,
b= 0.53,t(25) = 3.62,p=.001, with 34% of the variance explained (R 2 =.344) (see
figs. 4.19-4.21). Identification showed a positive but non-significant association,b= 0.40,
t(25) = 1.70,p=.102, accounting for roughly 10% of the variance (R 2 =.103). These
results suggest that participants’ subjective emotional and immersive experiences, partic-
ularly valence and immersion, are positively associated with perceived adaptability . No
significant relationship was found between profile and adaptivity nor adaptivity and the
interaction of profile and stress.

4.2.7 Post-Hoc Analysis
As a post hoc examination of trends, additional exploratory questions were assessed:
whether trial number influenced stress levels, and whether immersion was significantly
related to control and valence, since all factors independently predicted adaptivity . Va-

(SAM).
Trial number did not significantly predict stress,t=−0.56.

higher valence ratings associated with greater self-reported immersion (B= 0.38, p=
.008, ad justedR2 =.22). No significant relation was found between immersion and control,
with both directions of prediction yielding negligible effect sizes and p-values well above

A.5 Participant Recruitment
A.5.1 Recruitment Script
Participants were presented with the following recruitment text:
Hi! We are testing an interactive game where the narrative will be influenced
by your physiological responses and decisions as a suspect who is being inter-
rogated by the police.
There’s no right or wrong way to play; your playthrough gives us insight into
how people engage with dynamic narratives. The session will last approxi-
mately 25 minutes, including a short baseline recording and two brief ques-
tionnaires, and an interview at the end.
You will be questioned by the interrogator, who in real time reads your pulse
and how much you sweat to try and figure out if you are innocent or guilty .
These signals will influence how he reacts and how the narrative unfolds. There
are no wrong choices - it’s all part of the story .
The data is anonymous and used only for research purposes, and you can quit
at any time.
Do you wish to give it a try?
A.5.2 Baseline Procedure
Before the interactive experience, participants completed a baseline recording:
For the next minute, please sit quietly and relax. Try to breathe normally and
let your body settle. This gives us a baseline to understand your natural physi-
ological state before the interrogation starts.
A.5.3 Post-Experience Instructions
After completing the interactive narrative, participants were informed:
That’s the end of the interactive part. Now there is a short questionnaire and
we will have a brief interview afterward.
A.5.4 Post-Experience Questions
Participants were asked the following questions:
• What is your age?
• What is your gender?
• What did you think about the experience?
• Is there anything you would change to make the experience better?
• Is there anything you would like to add?
