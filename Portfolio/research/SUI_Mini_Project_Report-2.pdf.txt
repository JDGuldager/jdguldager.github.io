ABSTRACT
This project explores the design of an improved object interaction
technique for throwing in Virtual Reality. A force-based selection
and throwing mechanic was developed and compared to Unity’s
default interaction system through a small user evaluation. The
study combined performance-based measures with subjective feed-
back. Results indicate that the proposed interaction led to higher
task performance and was perceived as easier for hitting targets,
while both techniques were considered understandable and relatively
natural. The findings serve as an early usability assessment and
provide direction for further refinement of the interaction.

1 INTRODUCTION
One of the strengths of Virtual Reality (VR) is that it gives users the
freedom to interact with otherwise inaccessible and unnatural envi-
ronments in fun and entertaining ways. But there are some aspects
where the known and natural are the most desired and sometimes
hardest to achieve: for example physics. No matter how mystical the
surroundings, in most cases we want interactions to feel like they
do in our real world. Throwing is a task where we want it to feel as
natural as possible. [4] compared the task of throwing in VR to real
life and found that precision and accuracy were drastically worse

2 PREVIOUSWORK
Interaction in virtual environments has been widely studied within
the field of Spatial User Interfaces (SUIs), with a particular focus
on how users select, manipulate, and control virtual objects. This
section outlines the relevant academic foundations and practical
examples that inform the design of the project.
2.1 Selection and Manipulation in Virtual Environments
A fundamental framework for understanding interaction in virtual
environments is the distinction between selection and manipulation.
Selection refers to the process of identifying and choosing an object,
while manipulation involves modifying its position, orientation, or
other properties [2]. These two stages are often treated separately in
interface design but are closely related in practice.
In immersive environments, manipulation typically involves six
degrees of freedom (6-DoF), allowing objects to be translated and
rotated in three-dimensional space. Designing intuitive mappings
between user input and object behaviour is a central challenge, as
users must be able to control objects precisely while maintaining a
sense of natural interaction.
2.2 Extended Reach Interaction
One limitation of direct interaction techniques is that they are con-
strained by the user’s physical reach. To address this, several ex-
PAGE 2
tended interaction techniques have been developed that allow users
to interact with objects beyond arm’s length.
A well-known example is the Go-Go interaction technique pro-
posed by Poupyrev et al. [3]. This technique introduces a non-linear
mapping between the user’s physical hand movement and the virtual
hand position, effectively extending the reach of the user. When
the user’s hand moves beyond a certain threshold, the virtual hand
accelerates forward, allowing distant objects to be selected and ma-
nipulated.
This approach highlights an important design principle: interac-
tion techniques do not need to strictly mimic real-world behaviour,
but should instead support the user’s intention in an intuitive way.
The present project addresses a similar problem—interacting with
distant objects, but explores an alternative solution based on force-
driven attraction rather than spatial remapping.
2.3 Physics-Based Object Interaction
Another important direction in SUI research is the use of physics-
based interaction. Instead of directly attaching objects to the user’s
hand or controller, forces and torques are applied to simulate realistic
movement.
Physics-based approaches can enhance the perception of realism
by introducing properties such as inertia, momentum, and resistance.
This results in object behaviour that is less rigid and more dynamic
compared to direct manipulation techniques. However, it also intro-
duces challenges in control, as users must adapt to indirect responses
rather than immediate positional mapping.
In the context of this project, physics-based interaction is particu-
larly relevant because objects are not instantly moved to the user’s
hand. Instead, they travel through space toward the user, creating a
continuous transition between selection and grasping.
2.4 Game References
Two commercial VR games were used as design references for the
interaction concept:Acron: Attack of the Squirrels!andHalf-Life:
Alyx.
Acron: Attack of the Squirrels!demonstrates how simple grab-
and-throw mechanics can form the core of a VR interaction system.
The VR player interacts with the environment by grabbing nearby
objects and throwing them toward targets. The interaction is highly
intuitive, as it relies on familiar physical actions such as reaching,
aiming, and releasing. This makes it easy for users to understand
and engage with the system without requiring complex instructions.
Half-Life: Alyxintroduces a different approach through the use
of Gravity Gloves, which allow players to interact with objects at a
distance. Users can select an object, perform a pulling gesture, and
have the object travel toward their hand. This creates a smooth transi-
tion between distance selection and direct manipulation, maintaining
a strong sense of embodiment.
The present project combines these two interaction paradigms.
FromAcron, it adopts the simplicity and intuitiveness of grab-and-
throw mechanics. FromHalf-Life: Alyx, it adopts the force-based
attraction of distant objects. Additionally, the project extends these
ideas by introducing a recall mechanic, allowing objects to be
brought back to the user after being thrown, creating a continuous
interaction loop.
3 DESIGN ANDIMPLEMENTATION
The final product ended up being a small one minute game, where
users pick up and throw elemental balls, trying to hit as many targets
as possible. We wanted each different element (rock, fire, lightning)
to feel distinctive when interacting with them. First of all, a specific
set of haptic patterns were created for each element. Each contains
a haptic that loops when hovering the ball, one that plays when you
grab it and another looping pattern when holding the ball. All were
created using Meta Haptics Studio. This was chosen for its ability
to manually create advanced haptic patterns. However, integrating
this with XR Interaction Toolkit, which was used for the project,
proved to more than challenging than first anticipated. We created
the GetController Method (See figure 2) as a workaround to this
problem, which allowed for Meta haptic clips to be played on the XR
controllers. Most of the functionality was implemented by adding
the XRGrabInteractable to the balls, making it easy to connect
functionality to specific events (eg. hover, select enter / exit).
Figure 2: GetController method
We wanted a way to be able to select and grab the balls from
distance. This is inherently possible by hovering the ray on an
object, grabbing it with a button click and using the controller stick,
sliding it to your hand. However, through the grabbing interaction
we wanted to give the user a bit more sense of agency and physical
involvement in the virtual world. When hovering a ball, the user
can quickly pull their hand toward themselves to send the ball flying
into their hand. A ControllerData script tracks the velocity of both
controllers and detects whether it exceeds the set threshold. The ball
uses this information to activate the coroutine that moves the ball.
The coroutine uses a lerp to move the ball, and here ’t’ is controlled
by an animation curve. Each element implements its own custom
animation curve, making it possible to shape the flight path so it fits
the given element, giving them each a distinctive feel.
3.1 Improved Throwing Interaction
The throwing mechanic in the application is implemented as a cus-
tom system that extends beyond Unity’s default XR Interaction
Toolkit behavior. Instead of relying solely on controller velocity,
the system combines physical input with directional guidance to
improve accuracy and consistency.
When an object is released, the throw direction is calculated
using a projected target based on a crosshair (see Figure 3), which
is positioned in front of the non-throwing hand. This allows the
user to aim explicitly rather than depending only on natural hand
motion. The final velocity applied to the object is derived from
the controller’s movement magnitude, scaled and clamped within
defined limits to ensure stable and predictable behavior.

Additional adjustments, such as a slight upward bias and hor-
izontal correction, are applied to produce a more natural arc and
compensate for common misalignment between hand position and
player view. This results in a throwing interaction that feels both re-
sponsive and easier to control, particularly in target-based scenarios.
3.2 Evaluation Minigame
Targets are spawned at random positions within a defined radius
around the player, ensuring they appear within a controlled and
playable area. When a target is hit, it is immediately replaced by a
new one to maintain a consistent number of targets.
A time-limited scoring system is used to evaluate performance.
Players earn points by hitting targets and the final score is recorded
when the timer ends. These scores were used to compare Unity’s
default throwing system with the custom throwing mechanic, pro-
viding a simple measure of accuracy and effectiveness between the
two approaches.
4 EVALUATIONMETHOD
The evaluation is designed as a comparative user study between two
object interaction techniques in VR. The purpose is to compare the
proposed force-based selection and throwing mechanic with Unity’s
default object picking and throwing interaction.
The study follows a within-subjects design, meaning that each
participant tests both interaction techniques. This allows the perfor-
mance and experience of the two techniques to be compared using
the same participants, reducing differences caused by individual
skill level or prior VR experience.
4.1 Interaction Conditions
Two interaction conditions will be evaluated:
• Default Unity Interaction:Participants use the standard pick-
ing and throwing mechanic provided by Unity’s XR interaction
system.
• Proposed Force-Based Interaction:Participants use the cus-
tom interaction mechanic developed for this project, where
objects are thrown using the proposed throwing mechanic.
4.2 Task Design
Participants will complete the same target-hitting task using both
interaction techniques. In each condition, the participant will attempt
to hit as many targets as possible in one minute by selecting and
throwing objects within the virtual environment.
Each participant will complete the time based task with the default
Unity interaction and with the proposed force-based interaction. The
order of the two conditions should be counterbalanced, meaning
that some participants start with the Unity interaction while others
start with the proposed interaction. This is done to reduce learning
effects.
4.3 Measures
The evaluation will collect both performance-based and subjective
data.
Performance measuresinclude:
• Number of targets hit during the timed task
Subjective measuresinclude:
• Perceived ease of use
• Perceived control
• Perceived precision
• Did the interaction feel natural
• User preference between the two techniques in a number of
categories
These subjective measures will be collected through a short post-
task questionnaire after both conditions have been tested. The evalu-
ation questions and results can be seen in full on the project Github
page.
4.4 Procedure
Participants will first receive a short introduction to the VR environ-
ment and the task. Before each condition, they will be given a brief
practice period to become familiar with the interaction technique.
After the practice period, participants will perform the one minute
test using the first interaction technique. They will then repeat the
same task using the second interaction technique. The number of
successful hits will be recorded for each condition.
After completing both conditions, participants will be asked to
provide feedback on which interaction technique they found easier,
more accurate, more intuitive, more effortless and more enjoyable
to use.
4.5 Data Analysis
The collected data will be used to compare task results and subjective
perceptions for each interaction condition. The number of targets
hit will be compared between the default Unity interaction and the
proposed force-based interaction.
Qualitative feedback from participants will be used to identify
perceived strengths and weaknesses of each interaction technique.
5 RESULTS
5.1 Task Results
The performance results from the one-minute playthroughs are
shown in Figure 4. Two participants completed five trials with
each interaction technique. For both participants, the force-based
interaction resulted in a higher number of targets hit across all trials.
Across both participants, the force-based condition consistently
produced higher scores in every corresponding trial. The difference
between the two interaction techniques appears relatively stable,
with no instances where the default interaction outperformed the
force-based interaction.
These results indicate a higher task performance, measured by
number of targets hit, when using the force-based interaction com-
pared to the default Unity interaction.
5.2 Likert Scale Questionnaire
The Likert scale questionnaire results provide subjective evaluations
of the two interaction techniques across several dimensions. They
were asked to rate from a scale of 1 to 7. Results can be seen
visualized in Figure 5
For the default Unity interaction, both participants reported that
the interaction was easy to understand (scores of 7 and 6) and mod-
erately felt in control (both scoring 5). However, both participants
rated the ease of hitting targets relatively low (scores of 3 and 2) and
the perceived naturalness varied between participants (6 and 3) .
For the force-based interaction, both participants again rated the
interaction as easy to understand (both scoring 6 or 7) and reported
slightly higher perceived control (both scoring 6). Notably, both
participants rated the ease of hitting targets higher compared to the
default interaction (scores of 7 and 6). The perceived naturalness
was rated similarly or slightly higher (scores of 6 and 5) .
Additional questions specific to the force-based interaction
showed that pulling objects toward the user was rated as easy (scores
of 7 and 6), while the transition from pulling to throwing received

6 DISCUSSION
The evaluation compared the default Unity XR interaction with the
proposed force-based interaction using both performance measures
and subjective feedback. Overall, the results indicate a consistent
difference between the two techniques across both objective and
subjective data.
From a performance perspective, both participants achieved
higher scores in all trials when using the force-based interaction.
This suggests that the proposed interaction may support improved
accuracy in target-based tasks. The consistency of this pattern across
all trials and both participants strengthens the indication that the
difference is not due to isolated performances. However, the results
alone do not explain the reason for this improvement.
The subjective results provide additional context. Participants
rated both interaction techniques as easy to understand, indicating
that neither system posed a significant learning barrier. However, the
force-based interaction was rated higher in terms of ease of hitting
targets and slightly higher in perceived control. This aligns with
the observed performance differences and suggests that the added
directional guidance (e.g., crosshair-based aiming) may contribute
to improved usability.
The comparison questionnaire and qualitative feedback further
support this trend, with participants expressing a preference for
the force-based interaction, particularly in relation to targeting and
precision.
A key limitation of the evaluation is the small number of partici-
pants. With only two participants, the results cannot be generalized,
and individual differences may have a significant influence on the
outcome. A larger and more diverse participant group would be
required to draw more reliable conclusions.Additionally, the short
duration of each condition (one minute) also limits the ability to
assess long-term usability, fatigue or adaptation over time.
Rather than serving as a conclusive evaluation, this evaluation
design can be understood as an early-stage or mid-way usability test.
The purpose of the evaluation was not only to compare performance,
but also to identify strengths and weaknesses of the proposed inter-
action. In this context, the results are useful for informing further
development. For instance, while the force-based interaction appears
to improve targeting performance, the lower ratings for the transi-
tion between pulling and throwing highlight an area that requires
refinement.
Future evaluations should build on these findings by including
more participants, refining the interaction based on the identified
issues and potentially incorporating additional measures such as
accuracy rate or error patterns. This would allow for a more compre-
hensive understanding of how and why the interaction performs as
observed.
7 CONCLUSION
This project presented a force-based interaction technique for se-
lecting and throwing objects in VR and evaluated it against Unity’s
default interaction. The results showed improved performance and
more positive user feedback for the proposed method, particularly in
terms of target accuracy. However, limitations such as the small num-
ber of participants mean that the findings are not conclusive. Instead,
the evaluation highlights both the potential of the approach and areas
for improvement, supporting further iteration and development of
the interaction design.
