Abstract—This paper presents the design, implementation,
and evaluation of an embodied virtual reality (VR) experience
aimed at simulating mild altered states of consciousness (ASCs)
through non-human embodiment and respiration-driven biofeed-
back. Grounded in phenomenology, enactivism, and theories of
technological mediation, the experience places participants in
the role of a tree within a responsive forest ecosystem, where
breathing influences environmental growth through a mycor-
rhizal root network. The project explores how immersive VR may
support psychological processes associated with ASCs, including
nature connectedness, cognitive flexibility, and well-being. Us-
ing stylized visuals, applied-hallucination aesthetics, generative
sound, and meditative pacing, the experience was designed to
promote relational awareness and embodied engagement rather
than goal-oriented interaction. To evaluate the intervention,
40 participants were divided into a VR and a control group
and completed psychometric measures of nature connectedness,
cognitive flexibility, and affect. Quantitative analyses revealed no
significant differences between conditions, although participants
frequently reported feelings of relaxation, curiosity, and immer-
sion. Prior VR experience emerged as a significant predictor
of several outcome measures, suggesting that familiarity with
immersive environments may influence psychological responses.
The findings highlight both the potential and limitations of VR
as a tool for facilitating transformative experiences. While short-
term exposure did not produce significant measurable effects,
the study contributes an exploratory framework for designing
biofeedback-driven VR experiences that integrate embodiment,
altered perception, and ecological relational awareness.

I. INTRODUCTION
Over the course of our history, altered states of conscious-
ness (ASC) have drawn keen interest across human cultures
and in many forms, from trance induction and meditation to
sensory deprivation and the use of psychoactive substances
[1]. ASCs that are safely induced are associated with ex-
periences of deep connection and awe, increased creativity,
and a stronger motivation to act in favor of others and the
environment [2, 3]. In some psychoactive and meditation
contexts, they have been linked to long-term increases in
cognitive flexibility [4, 5, 6], directly linked to psychological
well-being and certain mental health outcomes [7, 8, 9, 10].
However, it is not a phenomenon that all have the ability
or access to experience because of health conditions, limited
access to training, social contexts, and societal disapproval of
psychoactive substances. As a result, practices like disciplined
mindfulness, meditation, or sessions are out-of-reach experi-
ences for many. VR technology mediation has recently shown
potential to induce mild ASC-like effects in a controlled and
safe environment [11, 12, 13, 14]. These also include addi-
tional effects on nature connectedness and cognitive flexibility
[13, 15, 16].
In this study we seek to outline a VR experience that is
to be used in this setting. The users will be put in the role
of a tree within a forest. By capturing breath signals through
a respiration sensor, their bodies will be allowed to become
causal agents in the ecosystem by sending electrical-like pulses
through a mycorrhizal root network. Their actions will nurture
the neighboring saplings, and they will experience how their
breath is nourishing the growth of the ecosystem. The design
of the study is grounded in phenomenological and embodied
theory. Instead of translating nonhuman sensory processes
literally, it maps them onto the participant’s own breath and
renders them through an applied-hallucination visual aesthetic.
In this paper we describe the theoretical framework, the design,
the implementation, and the evaluation.
II. BACKGROUND
A. Altered States, Cognitive Flexibility, Nature Connected-
ness, and Their Interrelation
1) Altered States of Consciousness:An altered state of con-
sciousness (ASC) is characterized by deviations in cognition,
perception, or experience that substantially differ from waking
norms and can be induced by physiological, psychological,
or pharmacological methods. Meditation, hypnosis, sensory
deprivation, dreaming, mystical experiences, and hallucinatory
states are all common ASCs [1].
ACSs can vary widely in valence and intensity. Some altered
states may be negative or destabilizing, such as psychotic
delirium, while others can be highly meaningful and transfor-
mative. The quality of an altered state is strongly influenced
by contextual factors such as set and setting [17], as well as
the individual’s sense of agency in entering the experience.
When intentionally and safely induced, ASCs may produce
a range of psychological benefits, including elevated mood,
feelings of awe and wonder, inner calm, increased creativity,
and greater philosophical insight. They can also involve a
sense of liberation from previous beliefs as set thought patterns
are temporarily opened up, allowing novel thinking to emerge
[18]. Afterwards, individuals may feel more curious, grateful,
and purposeful, with a stronger motivation to contribute to
others and the environment through empathy, compassion, and
a deeper connection to nature [2, 19, 20, 21].
2) Cognitive Flexibility and Positive Mental Functioning:
Cognitive flexibility (CF) is a person’s ability to go beyond
the immediately presented information, move between men-
tal processes, and the flexible use of prior knowledge in
changing environments [7]. Originally used as a term within
fields of expert knowledge acquisition and complex problem
solving [22], it has since been linked to a range of mental
processes, such as creative achievement, early reading skills,
and emotion regulation [23, 24, 25]. Lower CF is associated
with psychiatric conditions, including anxiety, impulsivity,
anorexia nervosa, and depression [8, 9]. This underscores the
importance of cognitive flexibility for prosperous living and
mental well-being.
Several practices known to induce altered states of con-
sciousness have also been associated with increased CF.
Regular meditators exhibit higher CF, possibly mediated by
increased attentional skills [4]. Similarly, successful interven-
tions with classic psychedelics have led to increased measures
of CF post-treatment even after first single use; their effect
partially explained by the loosening of rigid thought patterns
PAGE 3
associated with disorders such as depression and anxiety
[5, 6, 26].
Furthermore, recent studies link nature-relatedness to cog-
nitive flexibility [10]. Findings suggest that individuals with a
stronger connection to nature tend to exhibit greater CF, which,
in turn, supports a more adaptive appraisal of both their own
and others’ emotions. This mediating effect underscores how
nature-relatedness may indirectly enhance psychological well-
being by promoting flexible thinking patterns that improve
emotional regulation.
3) Relational Awareness and Self-Transcendence:Rela-
tional awareness, also referred to asself-transcendence, is
a pivotal mediating mechanism that facilitates the transition
from standard waking consciousness to a more expansive
state. It is defined by a cognitive and experiential shift in
which the person moves away from an individual-centered
perception toward asystem-centeredperception, experiencing
unity through a shift in self-perception, recognizing that they
are not separate from their embedded environment [27].
Means of self-transcendence include, among others, peak
experiences, altruism, and a spiritual connection to self, others,
nature, and the cosmos, achieved through practices such as
meditation, meaningful time spent in nature, sensory depriva-
tion, flow-state activities, and religious rituals—many of which
may also induce altered states of consciousness. According
to Maslow’s later theories, self-transcendence represents the
highest level of human motivation—the final stage in the
hierarchy of needs—serving as a catalyst for the subjective
experience of meaning in life [28, 29]. Going beyond the
self, in neuroscientific terms, means to abandon predetermined
frames of perception, specifically modulated by changes in
connectivity and activity in the Default Mode Network, a brain
region involved in self-referential processing [30, 31, 32].
Self-nature connection is defined as “the extent to which an
individual includes nature within his/her cognitive representa-
tion of self” [33], directly linking the two concepts together
through the shift in self-processing. Both altered states and a
higher sense of transcendence often observe an increased care
for the environment and feeling of belonging in the natural
world [34, 35, 36].
Relational awareness, or self-transcendence, serves as the
immediate precursor to a full altered state of consciousness and
to mystical experiences. This mechanism is the primary driver
for achieving long-term psychological benefits. By shifting the
perspective from ”self” to a state of unification with the world
or cosmos, self-transcendence promotes prosocial behavior,
environmental care, and empathy. Furthermore, by loosening
up set thought patterns through this self-transcendent shift, it
directly enhances cognitive flexibility and promotes general
psychological well-being [37, 18].
Together, these findings suggest that altered states of con-
sciousness may promote psychological well-being not only
through transient subjective experiences, but also through
enduring shifts in self-perception, cognitive flexibility, and
relational awareness that deepen one’s sense of connection to
others, nature, and the broader world. These insights shape
the psychological framework for the subsequent VR design,
informing how immersive experiences can be structured to
simulate altered states of consciousness and support well-
being through relational awareness, cognitive flexibility, and
connection to nature.
B. Phenomenological and Embodied Foundations
1) Umwelt:This study is based on the theory ofUmwelt
by von Uexk ¨ull where an organism’s senses, needs, and its
possible actions shade the world it inhabits. The organism
inhabits a species-specific perceptual world and not an ob-
jective reality available to all organisms [38, 39]. Uexk ¨ull
defines the notion of Umwelt as a world of experience in
contrast to the Umgebung, which is the physical surrounding
that is shared by organisms. He defines the Funktionskreis
as a dynamic loop where an organism’s field of action, the
Wirkwelt,and its perceptual field, the Merkwelt, are how the
organism’s world is enacted. This project uses a tree as the
base of the experience which is an organism influenced by
elements like photoperiod, light, soil nourishment, and fungal
elements. These elements can be defined as loosely analogous
to that of humans but lack a direct reference. By portraying
these elements as directly translated visualizations for a pure
human perception, we would reduce the tree’s Umwelt to
artificial elements like color fields or chemical gradients.
Instead, we seek to reorganize the perceptual experience of
the user through metaphorical mappings to dissolve the pure
human habitual view and create a sensorimotor system that
makes the tree’s Umwelt emerge. To achieve this, we seek to
incorporate a human bodily register, such as the autonomic
but voluntary physiological breath, to portray the processes
that are normally non-human.
2) Embodied Perception:Looking to Merleau-Ponty’s phe-
nomenology, we take that perception is an active, embodied
engagement with the world prestructured by the notion of the
body schema and not a brain-centric cognitive process based
on neural sensory input [40]. Instead of visualizing what a tree
would see, which would leave the user in a passive viewer
mode and thus leave the habitual human perception as is,
we seek to alter the user’s bodily activity. Introducing a total
perceptual collapse would bring disorientation and dissolve
the phenomenological meaning, so the change should act as
a partial dissolution by adapting perception through the users
audio-visual senses and using breathing as a causal act. The
experience should thus still have continuity and also portray
contingent habitual perception, giving the user insight into an
alternate way of perceiving as an organism.
3) Technological Mediation and Perceptual Breakdown:
Looking to the postphenomenology of Don Ihde, we see
that the field of technologically mediated perception can be
divided into different modes of how we experience our world:
embodiment relations, where the body schema incorporates
technology almost as a part of our bodies; hermeneutic rela-
tions, where information needs to be interpreted; and alterity
relations, where engagement takes on the quality of a quasi-
other [41, 42]. In Heidegger, we further see this dynamic in
PAGE 4
his distinction between the ready-to-hand, where the skillfully
used tool disappears and fades from our awareness, and the
present-at-hand, where the tool breaks down or resists use
and becomes obvious as an object [43]. In VR we conven-
tionally seek a stable and transparent embodiment relation.
In this study we seek a deliberate inducement of controlled
breakdown of the perceptual readiness-to-hand. This mediation
becomes perceptible and is felt but not fully interpretable
for the user, who is placed in a liminal in-between space
of embodied familiarity and alterity, where the users are
feeling radically different and partly estranged from their own
embodied expectations. With Heidegger’s notion ofStimmung,
the fundamental attunement in which our world and the way
of being-in-it are shaped, this suggests a focus on how the
experience is felt and not only on how it is seen.
4) Enaction and the Sensorimotor Loop:We move from
Ihde and Heidegger’s notion of technological mediation and
into an enactivist framework by Maturana and Varela’s theory
of autopoiesis [44]. Here the mediating technology does not
stand between the subject and the world but shapes the
cognitive dynamics of enaction. As Varela et al. point out,
cognition is not information processing but instead the contin-
uous enactment of a world through the structural coupling of
organisms [45].
The system of this study is based on a responsive sen-
sorimotor loop that is reacting to the user’s breathing and
head movement via a respiratory sensor and a head-mounted
display. The system senses and reacts to movements in a
continuous modulation of what the user is perceiving and how
the user moves or breathes next. This environmental dynamic
is created to evoke the temporalities similar to that of a tree
in a slower and more diffuse way.
5) The Limits of Cross-Species Imagination:With Nagel
and his critique of reductionist physicalism in his paper ”What
Is It Like to Be a Bat?” we recognize the epistemic limit
of being able to access the subjective experience of another
organism [46]. Here we see that what matters is not whether
trees possess phenomenal consciousness in a human sense,
but that any organisms experiential world is exclusive to
itself. With our study, we seek to explore the contingent and
embodied nature of human perception instead of creating a
tree simulator, in the notion that human perception is not
necessarily given in advance but shaped by embodied and
situated conditions. By circumventing didactic translations
and explanatory overlays, the experience seeks to avoid non-
diegetic explanation and relies on diegetic cues embedded in a
silent life-cycle structure of a tree that makes no verbal inter-
pretation and leaves the perceptual gap open by not resolving
it into information. Moreover, Eduardo Kohn contends that
semiosis is a characteristic of all living systems, rather than a
result of human language [47]. The field of plant neurobiology
research is to a high degree exploring forms of signaling and
cognition in plants and is increasingly discussed in relation
to theories of semiosis [48, 49]. Furthermore, by keeping
the environment’s aesthetic register organic and defined by
continuous morphogenesis and responsiveness, we seek to
portray this nonhuman meaning-making without domesticating
it with discrete and overly digital form.
Taking on another lifeform’s perspective falls under the
category of altered states of consciousness, as it is a fun-
damental departure from regular cognition and awareness.
This classification is not necessarily a definitive statement
on whether plants or other non-human entities possess con-
sciousness or not, as this is a much debated topic within
science; however, animism (the viewpoint that all entities have
sentience or mind), has been linked to a reduction of alienation
and newfound wonder, and is closely linked with altered states
of consciousness [3].
C. Biofeedback and Respiratory Interaction
Breathing operates simultaneously as atop-downand
bottom-upprocess. From a top-down perspective, intentional
control of the breath can influence neural oscillations, mus-
cle regulation, emotional state, and executive functioning;
practices such as yoga and martial arts frequently employ
controlled breathing to support attention and performance.
From a bottom-up perspective, breathing continuously adapts
subconsciously to bodily activity and environmental demands.
In this sense, respiration participates in an ongoing causal loop
between body and mind, making it a central component of
embodied cognition [50].
Disciplined methods of breath control, commonly referred
to as breathwork, are linked to altered states and positive
outcomes on cognitive flexibility, nature connectedness, and
self-referential processing [51, 52]. Breath also plays a central
role in many meditative traditions, where it functions as a
biological mediator for shifts in mental processing. By direct-
ing attention toward an otherwise automatic bodily process,
breath-focused practices may enhance interoceptive awareness
and bodily self-awareness [53].
Physiological measurements such as heart rate and respi-
ration are increasingly being used as input methods and for
evaluation purposes in VR [54]. These signals thus support an
embodied biofeedback system by making internal processes
perceptible through sensory feedback. As breathing lies be-
tween voluntary and involuntary control, it makes it suitable
as an interaction modality, as it is both deeply embodied and
cognitively accessible.
As a bodily process that continuously mediates between
internal states and environmental responsiveness, breath offers
a natural foundation for designing immersive experiences
aimed at facilitating altered modes of perception, relational
awareness, and connection to nature. By operating at the
boundary between conscious and unconscious control, self and
environment, breathing may also contribute to a temporary
loosening of rigid self-boundaries and habitual modes of self-
referential processing.
D. Virtual Reality as a Mediator
VR has shown recent potential in promoting a shift in
perspective-taking, mimicking altered states, and simulating
PAGE 5
non-human species embodiment, successfully facilitating ben-
eficial psychological effects on the user [55, 56, 13]. Although
a relatively new area of research, VR, due to its immersive,
”sensory-hijacking” properties, enables users to experience a
heightened sense of presence and temporarily adopt alternative
perceptual and cognitive frameworks in ways that conventional
media cannot. Virtual environments experienced through VR
headsets induce more awe and immersion than regular 2D
videos [57, 58]. Systematic reviews show that VR interven-
tions can be an accessible tool for stress reduction among
students and young adults [59], and clinical trials with VR
further suggest a favorable experience among patients for its
non-pharmacological interventions in reducing anxiety [60].
1) Virtual Reality and Altered States:Obvious parallels
between altered states and VR lie in the ability to tem-
porarily alter the senses beyond typical conscious experience.
”Cyberdelics”, the convergence of digital technologies and
psychedelic experiences originating in the late 1900’s, has
recently resurfaced alongside renewed interest in psychedelics
and virtual reality, raising questions about how insights from
psychedelic research, particularly the importance of cultural
context and “set and setting,” can shape the possibilities and
limitations of emerging mind-altering digital media [11, 61].
Hallucinations in VR increase cognitive flexibility, induce
a sense of awe, mystical experiences, and reduce physio-
logical arousal [13, 14]. Applied hallucinations to an im-
mersive environment causes similar cognitive alterations to
those reported under psychedelics, successfully inducing al-
tered states, scoring high on the ASC questionnaire nor-
mally used in psychedelic research, showing potential to
non-pharmacological or invasive simulation of hallucinogenic
phenomenology [12, 13, 62].
2) Virtual Reality and Embodiment:Embodying non-
human forms in VR has been shown to lead to increased
reflection on nature-self relationship and connection. A study
comparing immersive VR and desktop experiences of em-
bodying a tree found that greater immersion was linked to
stronger feelings of connection to nature. While VR did not
significantly increase nature-relatedness overall, participants in
the VR condition more often described the experience from
a first-person perspective and reflected on humanity’s rela-
tionship with nature, suggesting deeper identification and self-
reflection [15]. Another study where users embodied animals
in immersive virtual environments created stronger connec-
tion to nature than watching the same experience on video.
This increased sense of interconnection also led to greater
awareness of environmental risks and stronger environmental
engagement, with effects lasting up to a week [55].
3) Simulated Nature Environments:The combination of
meditation and nature immersion heightens self-nature inter-
connectedness more than either alone, bringing virtual natural
environments into relevance [63]. VR nature environments can
improve well-being, relaxation, and emotional states, provid-
ing a foundation for designing calming VR experiences and
suggesting VR can substitute real nature when inaccessible
[16, 64].

The working assumption is that immersive VR experiences
may influence well-being through a sequence of experien-
tial and cognitive shifts. Specifically, altered or intensified
experiential states of relational awareness and non-human
embodiment may facilitate more flexible patterns of thinking
and a shift in self-cognition, which in turn support improved
emotional processing and a stronger sense of connection to
nature. This study aims to advance VR research and deepen
understanding of the interconnection among altered states,
mental flourishing, and the affordances of VR.
III. RESEARCHQUESTION
Can an embodied, nature-based VR experience that sim-
ulates an altered state of consciousness produce measurable
positive effects on nature connectedness, cognitive flexibility
and psychological well-being?
IV. DESIGN
A. Design Goals
Guided by the theoretical framework, three goals shaped the
design process: inducing mild altered states through perceptual
transformation, facilitating non human embodiment through
a tree perspective, and supporting psychological processes
related to cognitive flexibility and nature connectedness.
The design principles derived from the framework are
shown in Fig. 1. Gradual visual and auditory morphologies
transition participants into a non human perceptual frame with-
out overwhelming stimulation. Breathing acts as the primary
interaction modality, coupling participants to environmental
change to support relational awareness. The experience fur-
ther emphasizes meditative pacing, cyclical progression, and
artistic abstraction rather than goal oriented interaction or
photorealism.
B. Narrative Loop
The experience follows a cyclical structure consisting of a
seed phase, gradual growth into tree embodiment, breathing
interaction, culmination of forest growth, and return to the
seed state. The transition into tree embodiment is represented
through gradual upward movement during growth. The loop
intentionally avoids explicit completion states, aligning with
PAGE 6
ecological cycles and meditative practices emphasizing pro-
cess over goals.
Environmental progression is driven entirely by breathing
interaction, while the absence of objectives supports mindful-
ness principles of presence and non-striving.
C. Visual Design
The visual design adopts a low poly artistic aesthetic rather
than photorealism, informed by findings from the pre-design
survey presented in Section VI-A, which indicated preference
toward stylized experiences and aversion to realistic replication
of nature.
Visual morphologies follow an “applied hallucination”
model [12, 13], embedding perceptual distortions within
coherent environments rather than relying on conventional
psychedelic aesthetics. Tree textures were generated in
TouchDesigner, while sky morphologies were implemented
through Unity shaders. These transformations function as the
primary driver of altered perception.
D. Interaction and Biosignal Design
Breathing is mapped to a visual root network, establishing
a “root brain” metaphor linking plant physiology and human
bodily awareness. Breath-driven root pulses propagate through
the environment and stimulate nearby tree growth, creating a
biofeedback loop between participant physiology and environ-
mental change.
The pulsating roots evoke familiar biological metaphors
such as blood flow, neural signaling, and pulmonary anatomy,
creating intuitive links between human and plant systems while
reinforcing relational awareness.
E. Embodiment Design
The embodiment design moves beyond visual observa-
tion toward experiential re-embodiment. Participants gradually
adopt a less anthropomorphic perspective through growth and
environmental coupling rather than locomotion.
This approach aligns with findings suggesting that non
human embodiment may strengthen nature connectedness and
perspective taking abilities [15, 55]. The feedback loop be-
tween participant input and environmental response further
reflects enactive cognition principles [45].
F . Sound Design
Sound was treated as a co-evolving experiential layer to
support immersion and altered perception. The soundscape
consists of slow moving electronic ambient elements designed
to promote calmness through gradual sound onsets, lower
articulation, slower tempo, and lower registers [65].
Structured audio elements were avoided in favor of unobtru-
sive sound design supporting a meditative “zen” atmosphere
[66]. Prior VR work further indicates that mismatched audio
may disrupt contemplative experiences [67].
Breathing controls sound levels, sequencing, and gradual
introduction of layers, reinforcing the cyclical progression of
the experience. In this way, sound is not used as soundtrack,
but as an additional layer that supports immersion by mapping
the physical process of breathing to elements of the sonic
world of the experience.
G. Mindfulness Setting
The experience was designed to align with mindfulness
principles without explicit instruction. Breathing serves as the
attentional anchor, while the absence of goals and repetitive
cyclical interaction support sustained presence.
Research on altered states highlights the importance of set
and setting, both mental and physical, in shaping subjec-
tive experience and for facilitating beneficial transformation
[17]. Consequently, the design avoids abrupt transitions and
overstimulation while maintaining gradual perceptual change
while aiming to lower the border to altered experiences by
decoupling it from a religious, new-age, or spiritual context.
H. Qualitative Pre-Design Research
Although VR shows potential for influencing psychological
states, less attention has been given to audience expecta-
tions and receptivity. Serious games must balance engagement
with transformational goals [68], while avoiding researcher-
audience misalignment [69].
Therefore, a pre-design survey was conducted to examine
attitudes toward VR, well being, nature exposure, and per-
ceived connection to nature.
The survey adopted an exploratory user centered approach
to identify design strategies likely to support engagement and
positive emotional outcomes. Results are presented in Section
VI-A.
V. IMPLEMENTATION
The aim of this section is to lay out the technical implemen-
tations that were carried out to create a coherent experience
that matched our design goals. Full description of the project
implementation can be found in Appendix A - Worksheet.
A. Respiration Data
A core component of the system was the acquisition and
integration of live respiration data (Figure 2). Respiration was
measured using the BioSignalsplux Piezo-Electric Respiration
(PZT) sensor, a wearable chest belt designed to detect thoracic
or abdominal displacement caused by breathing cycles [70].
The sensor data was streamed in real time to a PC via
Bluetooth using the OpenSignals application, which provides
normalized respiration values typically ranging from approxi-
mately -1.5 to 1.5 regardless of user body size. This normal-
ization simplified both cross-user consistency and testing, as
recalibration between participants was unnecessary.
To integrate the live biosignal data into the VR application,
the LSL4Unity package was imported into the Unity project.
Using the package’s sample implementation for receiving Lab
Streaming Layer (LSL) input as a foundation, the provided
scripts were adapted to interface with the OpenSignals respi-
ration stream. This resulted in a single continuously updated
float value representing the user’s respiration state, which
could then be accessed by other scripts to drive real-time
environmental interactions.

B. Environment
Figure (3) shows an image of the environment, including
trees, skybox, terrain and roots.
1) Trees:Trees serve as a central visual element in the
experience, representing both the player and the surrounding
ecosystem. A single procedural tree model, created in Blender,
was used as the basis for all trees in the environment. The
model was constructed with a simple low-poly workflow and
shaded in Unity using a custom shader to achieve a stylized
appearance.
To support dynamic growth, the tree model was rigged
with an armature, enabling branch growth by scaling bones
through animation. The growth speed of the surrounding trees
is directly controlled by live respiration data, with exhalation
increasing the animation speed to create the impression that
the player’s breath restores the environment.
The surrounding forest uses multiple instances of the same
tree model. A central forest controller script manages when
trees are activated based on each tree’s distance from the
player, creating a wave-like outward growth effect. To reduce
visual repetition, tree instances were given randomized rota-
tions and material variations.
Each tree responds to delayed respiration data, allowing
more distant trees to react slightly later than nearby ones.
This temporal offset reinforces the perception that the player’s
breathing gradually propagates restoration throughout the
wider ecosystem.
2) Terrain and Visual Effects:The environment was con-
structed using Unity’s terrain system, populated with custom
vegetation assets including trees, grass, flowers, and mush-
rooms to create a dense stylized ecosystem. To maintain
VR performance, vegetation placement was restricted to the
playable area, concentrating visual detail where interaction
occurs.
Custom shaders were developed to support the aesthetic
identity of the experience. An animated bark effect was created
for the trees using a sprite sheet–based flipbook shader, derived
from generative visuals originally produced in TouchDesigner.
This approach was chosen over video textures due to UV
mapping limitations on the low-polygon tree models and the
performance issues associated with multiple video-rendered
materials.
Additional visual feedback was implemented through a
custom full-screen URP post-processing shader that applies
pixelation, posterization and procedural noise. Live respiration
data modulates scene brightness in real time, strengthening the
connection between the user’s breathing and the environmental
atmosphere.
To further enhance the surreal visual style, a custom skybox
shader was developed to produce animated kaleidoscopic
effects through dynamic cubemap distortion and color modu-
lation, contributing to the immersive and psychedelic aesthetic
of the experience.
3) Roots:A custom emissive shader was developed to vi-
sualize the mycorrhizal root network as a breathing-responsive
environmental effect. During exhalation, a soft pulse of green
light travelled along the root geometry, briefly revealing the
underground network and reinforcing the perception of real-
time environmental responsiveness.
The effect was driven by live respiration data processed in
Unity, where breath state and amplitude were continuously
passed to the shader. Exhalation events triggered animated
light pulses along the mesh, shaped with smooth transitions
and subtle procedural variation to create a more organic
appearance. A low ambient emission was maintained between
pulses to preserve visibility in the VR environment. The
texture used for the ground was made slightly transparent,
to render the root system beneath the ground visible for the
player.
C. Timeline
The progression of the experience was primarily managed
through a central lifecycle controller script, which coordinated
transitions between different stages of the experience. Key
functionality was triggered using animation events, allowing
precise synchronization between interactive logic and visual
sequences. The introductory sequence was implemented using
Unity’s Timeline system, enabling coordinated control of
player movement, lighting changes, and environmental events.
Timeline-based animation blending was particularly important
in VR, as it ensured smooth transitions and avoided abrupt
scene changes that could disrupt immersion or user comfort.
PAGE 8
D. VR and Test Optimization
To maintain stable VR performance, several optimization
strategies were implemented, including restricting terrain de-
tail and vegetation to the playable area, applying LOD systems
and culling for environmental assets, and adopting a low-poly
visual style. Custom shaders were optimized to reduce ren-
dering overhead, replacing more expensive techniques where
necessary. In some cases, visual fidelity was intentionally re-
duced to ensure consistent performance on the target hardware.
To support efficient development and playtesting, a session
controller was also implemented, allowing the experience to be
paused, started, and restarted directly during runtime, enabling
faster iteration without repeatedly relaunching the application
for each new test participant.
E. Sound - Pure Data
The Pure Data patch receives breath input via OSC. The
input is split into two streams: One stream tracks the input,
the second stream keeps track of whether the user is in- or
exhaling. In this way, different synth engines can be mapped
to breathing in, or out. The synthesis section consists of
multiple synth engines which combine aspects of Amplitude
Modulation and Frequency Modulation to give cyclical motion
to the soundscape. Additionally, slow arpeggios and generative
melodies are used to accompany breathing out. Over time,
more and more synth engines are activated and added to the
mix, mirroring the growing forest. Low-pass filtering is applied
to give the sound a warm quality. A comparatively low register
scale forms the basis of all drones, melodies and arpeggios,
ensuring overall harmonic consonance. All individual sound
levels are mapped to breath input, which creates a slow and
serene ambient soundscape.
VI. METHODS
A. UX Pre-Design Survey
In total, 22 participants (15 male, 7 female; mean age
= 28.36, SD = 12.1) completed the online survey. To as-
sess participants’ relationship with natural environments, two
multiple-choice questions and four 7-point Likert-scale items
were included. Four items measured mindfulness and stress,
each rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Four Likert-scale items
assessed participants’ comfort with different VR design ap-
proaches relevant to the project. An additional question ex-
amined expectations regarding a nature-based VR experience.
The full list of questions can be found in Appendix B.
Survey responses regarding comfort levels in VR expe-
riences indicated that participants were most receptive to
“seeing the world from a non-human perspective” (M =
5.27/7). “Engaging in guided meditative experiences” received
an average rating of 4.59, while “moving slowly or staying still
for long periods” scored 4.18. The option participants felt least
comfortable with was “experiencing your body as something
non-human (e.g., a tree)”, which had the lowest mean score
of 3.63 (See Fig. 5).
When asked about expectations for a nature-focused VR
game, the majority of respondents highlighted curiosity and

riences (63.6%). Emotional connection was selected far less

B. Questionnaire Design
Psychometric self-report measures were designed to capture
both outcomes and the psychological mechanisms underlying
the VR experience. Based on the foundational theoretical
framework, it assumes that immersive, embodied, nature-based
VR can affect users through a chain of processes involving
altered experiential states, embodied cognition, and increased
relational awareness, leading to changes in cognitive flexibility,
emotional processing, and greater nature-connectedness.
The questionnaire was implemented in PsychoPy [72] with
Python and consisted of 22 questions across three main
themes. In addition to the quantitative measures, participants
were asked about gender, age, and VR experience. No other
personal or identifiable data was collected.
For clarity and methodological rigor, the final questionnaire
concentrates on three core domains, using validated measures
to ensure reliability and comparability across studies. Four
items on a 7-point Likert scale measurednature connected-
ness, eight 7-point Likert scale items measuredcognitive flex-
PAGE 9
ibility, and ten items on a 5-point Likert scale pinpointedwell-
beingthrough positive and negative affect scores. Development
of the quantitative questionnaire followed a construct-driven,
multi-source integration approach. The questions were adapted
from The Extended Inclusion of Nature in Self scale (EINS,
[33]) and shortened versions of the Cognitive Flexibility In-
ventory (CFI, [73]) and Positive and Negative Affect Schedule
(PANAS, [74]), selected on their psychometric robustness,
previous validation, and capability of capturing short-term
effects rather than general personality traits. See the full list
of questions in Appendix C.
1) Nature Connectedness:The Inclusion of Nature in Self
Scale (INS, [75]) is a single-item visual scale of circles labeled
”self” and ”nature” that overlap to varying degrees, used to
measure and compare connectedness to nature across cultures
and people with little to no need for explanation or translation.
The Extended Inclusion of Nature in Self scale (EINS, [33]) is
an updated version of the INS, including three additional items
that rely on spatial graphic metaphors of nature connection,
and has shown to be psychometrically stronger than the orig-
inal one-item scale. The conciseness and easy-to-understand
implementation made the EINS the most relevant scale for the
current scope of capturing transient, subtle states. Additionally,
it was favored for its ability to capture a relational dimension
of oneness or separateness through lines and/or circles that
convey size, distance, or centrality, aligned with the relevant
construct of relational awareness.
2) Cognitive Flexibility:Cognitive flexibility emerged as
a critical construct to evaluate in this project, serving as a
key mediating mechanism by linking experiential states to
longer-term psychological outcomes. Traditional behavioral
measures of cognitive flexibility, such as the Stroop task
or Alternative Uses Task, showed weak group differences
in similar contexts or demanded precision and time while
relying on partly subjective evaluation [13, 76]. Therefore, a
self-report approach was adopted. The Cognitive Flexibility
Inventory (CFI, [73]) conceptualizes cognitive flexibility as
the ability to perceive alternative options, generate multiple
solutions, and reframe situations adaptively. Across validation
studies, the scale has shown strong reliability and construct
validity, with higher scores consistently associated with lower
depression, reduced psychological distress, and more adaptive
coping strategies. Due to practical considerations and the
need for efficient administration, an 8-item shortened version
of the original 20-item CFI was used in this study while
retaining its core conceptual dimensions of control perception
and alternative solution generation.
3) Psychological Well-Being:A shortened version of the
Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS, [74]) was
used to assess psychological well-being, as it is well-suited for
capturing immediate, short-term affective states in comparison
to other psychological instruments focused on general charac-
teristics. The PANAS consists of single-word items assessing
different psychological states, organized into two-factor struc-
tures of positive and negative affect. Validation studies indicate
that PANAS maintains strong psychometric properties, with
good internal consistency and measurement invariance across
demographic groups. Importantly, the PANAS is robust in
reflecting internal states rather than objective indicators of
quality of life, and is relevant given the brevity of the project
[77]. Ten out of the twenty PANAS terms were chosen for the
final questionnaire.
C. Participants
In total, 40 participants took part in the testing (14 Female,
26 Male; mean age = 24.83, SD = 2.47). The present study
employed an A/B testing framework to isolate the effects of
VR. 20 participants experienced the VR simulation and com-
pleted a questionnaire afterward, while 20 in the control group
only completed the questionnaire. The mean VR experience of
the participants was 2.4 out of 5 (SD = 1.39); slightly higher
in the VR intervention group (M = 2.9) than in the control
group (M = 1.9).
D. Test Procedure
Participants were recruited at Aalborg University Copen-
hagen campus by convenience sampling. The control group
completed the questionnaire individually on a researcher-
provided laptop. Participants assigned to the VR condition
received a short briefing prior to the experience. Thereafter,
they were seated in a chair, fitted with a breath sensor, and
instructed to breathe deeply and steadily while a baseline
measurement was recorded. They were then given a VR
headset and headphones and instructed to relax and observe
the experience without pursuing any specific goal. After the
VR experience ended, participants completed the questionnaire
on a laptop. Both groups received a nominal non-monetary
incentive following participation.
E. Data Analysis
Statistical analysis was conducted in R [78] using R Studio
[79].
Participant-level positive and negative affect scores were
calculated by separately summing the relevant items, while
cognitive flexibility and nature connectedness scores were
calculated by averaging item responses. Scores were subse-
quently rescaled to additional variables to improve compara-
bility across measures in the statistical models. The total of all
scaled items formed an overarching outcome variable at the
participant level.
The primary analysis focused on comparisons between the
control and intervention groups across the main outcome
domains: nature connectedness, cognitive flexibility, and psy-
chological well-being. Normalized outcome variables were
used in the multivariate analyses, while the unscaled, separate
values were used in the t-tests.

VIII. DISCUSSION
The present study examined whether a nature-based, em-
bodied VR intervention mimicking traditional altered states in-
fluenced cognitive flexibility, well-being, and nature connect-
edness compared to a control condition. Across independent-
samples t-tests and multivariate analyses, no significant effects
of condition were observed. Although a trend suggested lower
negative affect in the VR group, this difference did not reach
statistical significance.

Fig. 7. Linear models of the outcomes as a function of VR experience
One interpretation is that the mechanisms underlying cogni-
tive flexibility, nature connectedness, and psychological well-
being are relatively stable and may require long-term or
repeated interventions. In related VR experiments, exposure of
up to 18 minutes led to significant phenomenological changes
[12], more than twice the exposure provided by the present
design. For exposure therapy, repeated weekly sessions of up
to 15 minutes each showed to be the most effective [80]. This
aligns with the idea that VR’s impact depends on the depth of
presence and the extent to which habitual cognitive-emotional
patterns are disrupted.
In contrast, VR experience emerged as a more stable
predictor of both cognitive flexibility and negative affect,
suggesting a familiarity or adaptation effect. This implies
that immersive environments may elicit different levels of
novelty-related cognitive load depending on prior exposure,
with inexperienced users showing greater novelty sensitivity.
This introduces a disruptor: VR is not a neutral medium
across participants. Condition effects can be partially masked
or distorted by differences in familiarity. This indicates a
necessity to control for individual experience differences in
future works. Similarly, other studies found that VR experience
impacts the results and perception of the virtual experience
[16].
Considering free-form feedback, however, the simulation
successfully made participants feel relaxed and curious, often
leading them to report a high sense of immersion. Taken
together, these points mainly point to faults in exposure dosage
and participant sample size rather than in the technological
PAGE 11
design.
The questionnaire could also attract skepticism: It is unclear
whether the selected questions were sufficient to capture the
complex mental states targeted. A wide range of measurement
techniques exists for the same constructs, with psychological
concepts often overlapping, making it difficult to confidently
determine what is truly captured versus what one expects
to capture. Similar studies found that VR overall did not
significantly increase nature-relatedness, though higher immer-
sion led to deeper reflection on their relationship with nature
[15]. This suggests that more elaborate questions could reveal
effects that are currently not captured.
The transition enacted by the seed-to-tree-to-fruit sequence
extends the participant’s point of view from inhabiting the
stages of the organism as a whole and on to become a falling
fruit: A transient part of the lifecycle instead of the whole tree
system, thereby altering how self, environment, and process
are experienced in relation to one another. Focusing on cogni-
tive flexibility, well-being, and nature connectedness–although
causally related to changes in self-processing–excludes evalu-
ating possible effects on embodied phenomenology.
Additionally, the mental concepts presently targeted are
closely related and often causally intertwined. A more con-
centrated approach might fit better, targeting only one concept
at a time with longer, in-depth qualitative research; extending
the questionnaire to the sources’ full list of items, or comple-
menting the data with behavioral tasks or biological data.
A. UX Design and VR For Transformational Games
The UX survey revealed greater comfort with a non-human
perspective, whereas experiencing the body as non-human was
unfavorable. This suggests that adopting an alternative per-
spective may feel easier than fully inhabiting it. As a result, the
design should prioritize changes in awareness and viewpoint
rather than emphasizing physical embodiment. Research on
transformational games aligns with this interpretation, propos-
ing that experience and identity operate as separate dimensions
of transformation. Players may be more drawn to altering how
they perceive and interpret the world than to changing their
sense of self or bodily identity [68]. These findings support the
design choice of an ”incidental” embodiment through breath
and shift of perception through auditory and visual means
instead of requiring the user to adopt specific movements or
simulate inhabiting the body of another species.
The survey further confirmed that a stylized adaptation
rather than realistic visuals is the most favorable approach
when designing nature-based simulations, as”something un-
usual and artistic”emerged as one of the two highest scoring
options on what one hopes to gain from a nature-based
VR experience, further supported by the comment from one
participant stating they may be negatively primed if it was
to imitate nature. This aligns with art game theory, where
striving for photorealism can lead to a quality barrier, while
an unusual or artistic aesthetic approach can hone expressive
potential and a more metaphorical mapping; in this case, of-
fering an aesthetic, meditative experience through abstraction
and symbolism [81].Curiosity/exploration, the other highest-
scoring wish for the experience, raises questions about future
design paths. Exploration and curiosity could be understood
as intellectual curiosity, in that case aligning with the design,
or as literal exploration in an adventure-type game. Future
work should define expectations by revising the UX research
questions to refine the narrative and interaction design and un-
cover target group preferences. Other restorative virtual natural
environments have, comparatively, elicited feedback in favor
of more exploration and interaction [82]. In an exploratory
study on design for psychedelic VR experiences, unstructured
interviews revealed that participants wanted more interactivity
and felt trapped or bored. Suggestions also mentioned wanting
to smell or feel the particles, implying that engaging more
senses than visual and auditory simulation could improve
immersion and thereby heighten psychological outcomes [67].
B. Limitations and Further Research
Given the modest sample size, the study may have been
underpowered to detect small-to-medium effects. Additionally,
resource constraints limited the exposure time for the VR
experience and the length of the questionnaire. Furthermore,
reliance on self-reported measures may have limited the ac-
curacy of the findings, particularly for constructs such as
cognitive flexibility, where subjective assessments may not
fully capture actual changes or subtle differences between pre-
and post-experience states.
Brief VR exposure alone may not be sufficient to alter
cognitive flexibility or affective state in a meaningful way,
whereas prior VR experience appears more consistently related
to emotional and cognitive responding. This points toward
the importance of considering user history and adaptation
processes in immersive technology research, rather than treat-
ing VR exposure as a uniform experimental stimulus. Longer
exposure and follow-up questions a week post-exposure could
further reveal patterns. Measurement of physiological states,
such as breathing patterns, could reveal additional effects.
Due to limited computational power, a stop-motion ap-
proach was adopted for the texture morphologies, resulting in
a less natural, hallucination-true animation rather than smooth,
flowing movement. This might have impacted the effect of the
applied hallucinations. Future work should refine the visual
morphology model.
Lastly, refining and extending the qualitative post-
questionnaire, alongside the UX survey, could provide a
clearer understanding of the underlying psychological mech-
anisms at play and help determine whether increased inter-
action depth is desirable. This may also inform whether the
experience should be expanded beyond its current modality
to incorporate additional sensory channels, such as the olfac-
tory, thereby enriching embodiment and supporting a more
nuanced exploration of perception and presence. Furthermore,
the project’s limited time frame did not allow for iterative
playtesting of alternative approaches to audio and visuals.
Development decisions based on user insights instead of
PAGE 12
developer assumptions could yield stronger transformational
outcomes [68].
IX. CONCLUSION
At the forefront of virtual reality lies its potential for
transformation, particularly through its capacity to reshape
cognition, self-perception, and sensory processing. This be-
comes especially significant in contexts where limited access
to restorative environments or altered states of experience
restricts opportunities for psychological renewal. Yet, the same
novelty that makes VR compelling also reveals clear gaps
in empirical understanding, highlighting the need for more
rigorous, nuanced, and longitudinal research into its effects
and design principles.
This study should be understood as an exploratory foun-
dation for designing psychologically impactful virtual expe-
riences through integrating psychology, user research, neuro-
science, and technological affordances, rather than a definitive
evaluation of their efficacy. While quantitative measures did
not yield strong statistical significance, qualitative findings
— including pre-design target group insights and open-ended
post-experience feedback — consistently indicated height-
ened immersion and perceived relaxation that standardized
instruments failed to fully capture. This divergence suggests
a limitation not necessarily in the intervention itself, but in
the sensitivity of current evaluative frameworks to subtle,
subjective, and phenomenological shifts, as well as likely
being a product of a conservative sample size and limited
exposure.
Taken together, the findings point to a broader methodolog-
ical and conceptual challenge: the need to better align mea-
surement tools with lived, embodied experience in immersive
environments. Rather than viewing the results as inconclusive,
they can be interpreted as an invitation to further develop both
design approaches and research paradigms in VR and serious
games, particularly those aimed at facilitating meaningful
psychological transformation.
