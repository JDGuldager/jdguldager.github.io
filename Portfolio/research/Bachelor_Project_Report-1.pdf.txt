Abstract:
This project explores the design, development, and
evaluation of a Virtual and Augmented Reality
(VR/AR) system aimed at supporting balance train-
ing in elderly users with vestibular dysfunction. By
leveraging interactive design principles and gamified
elements, such as a frog guiding users across lily pads
on a virtual lake, the system seeks to make reha-
bilitation more engaging and intuitive. The project
integrates three core design perspectives: informa-
tion, interaction, and presentation design, to cre-
ate a user-centred experience that facilitates motor
learning. A key aspect of the study involved com-
paring the user experience and perceived effective-
ness of AR and VR modalities to understand their
respective benefits and limitations in a rehabilita-
tion context. A mixed-methods evaluation, includ-
ing expert feedback, user interviews, and standard-
ised questionnaires, was conducted to assess usabil-
ity , engagement, and therapeutic potential. Results
indicate a generally positive reception from partici-
pants, with VR being perceived as more immersive
and engaging, while AR was viewed as less disorient-
ing and more familiar. However, challenges related
to tracking accuracy , hardware limitations, and bal-
ancing cognitive load emerged across both modali-
ties. The project demonstrates the feasibility of using
XR technologies in vestibular rehabilitation and of-
fers a foundation for future iterations focused on im-
proved hardware integration, expanded user testing,
and long-term clinical validation.

1 Motivation and Inspiration for the Project: Transition from
the Previous Semester’s Project
In the previous semester, our group started exploring use of Virtual Reality in a rehabilitation
setting as part of our semester project. We investigated existing challenges for post-operative
shoulder patients during their rehabilitation process, with a particular focus on the difficul-
ties encountered during at-home training. Our findings revealed that high costs and limited
time resources in the public healthcare sector restricted the availability and frequency of su-
pervised physiotherapy sessions. This left patients to perform their rehabilitation exercises
at home, where inconsistency and lack of motivation could hinder their progress. Moreover,
the absence of immediate feedback during unsupervised sessions could lead to incorrect ex-
ercise execution, reducing their overall effectiveness, and potentially prolonging recovery
times.
In attempt to address these challenges, we developed and evaluated a non-gamified VR ap-
plication designed to complement traditional physiotherapy in a home setting, showcased in
Figure 1.
Figure 1: A screenshot from last semester’s application in action, demonstrating the virtual
environment, virtual mirror and physiotherapist avatar (Guldager et al., 2025).
The application was set in a virtual environment that resembled a typical physiotherapy stu-
dio, with the aim of providing a familiar training atmosphere for the users. A key feature of
this prototype was a virtual mirror, enabling users to visually monitor their movements in real
time while receiving multimodal feedback through visual and auditory cues, as well as haptic
feedback via controller vibrations. To further support correct execution, a virtual physiother-

apist character was incorporated to demonstrate each exercise prior to its execution, helping
to assure proper form and technique.

The application included two customisable exercises, where users could adjust the number

The results of our experiment revealed that the application was generally well-received by

and helped shape the direction for future work.
As part of the project, we also established our first collaborative relationships with external

nership enabled us to adapt our application to real-world rehabilitation needs, ensuring that
it remained both relevant and practically applicable. Moreover, it offered us important insight
into how such development processes unfold within professional settings, thus extending our
experience beyond the purely academic context.

In connection with our growing knowledge within the use of VR in healthcare and educa-

Following the initiation of the collaboration, we held an introductory meeting with Kasper

gies and encouraged us to expand our research scope to include both VR and AR, with the
aim of assessing their potential benefits and challenges in rehabilitation applications.
All in all, the meeting served as a foundational basis for our subsequent work and played a
central role in shaping the research question that we will investigate in greater depth through-
out the project.

2 Introduction
Balance disorders caused by vestibular dysfunction are a significant health concern for the
elderly , often leading to dizziness, instability , and a heightened risk of falls. These issues can
severely impact independence and quality of life. While rehabilitation plays a crucial role
in managing balance problems, traditional methods can lack engagement, motivation, and
accessibility for older adults. This highlights a need for innovative, user-centred approaches
that combine clinical effectiveness with an engaging experience.
This project explores the potential of Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR) as
tools for vestibular rehabilitation. Through a playful, immersive stepping task, where users
follow a guiding frog across lily pads on a virtual lake, the system aims to make balance train-
ing more intuitive and enjoyable. The design integrates information, interaction, and presen-
tation principles to address the specific needs of elderly users and support motor learning
and spatial awareness.
A key focus of the project is comparing AR and VR modalities to understand their respective
strengths, limitations, and impact on user experience. The system was developed in Unity
using the Meta Quest 3 headset, with several foot-tracking methods tested. The final solution
relied on repurposing the Quest 3 controllers, mounted to the user’s feet. Testing involved
elderly participants with no prior VR/AR experience, using a mixed-methods approach that
combined interviews, expert feedback, and standardised questionnaires.
This report documents the full development process, from design and implementation to eval-
uation. It aims to contribute insights into Mixed Reality (XR) based rehabilitation, highlight-
ing design strategies, user engagement, and practical considerations for immersive healthcare
technologies. Ultimately , the project demonstrates the potential of XR technologies to create
meaningful, user-centred rehabilitation experiences for elderly users.
2.1 Research Area
"A comparative analysis and feasibility-study of VR and AR that support elderly vestibular
patients in performing reactive stepping exercises for physiotherapy"
This question comprises several key components, each of which defines the scope and intent
of the project:

Comparative Analysis of VR and AR
The project seeks to compare Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR) platforms as
mediums for delivering physiotherapeutic interventions. Specifically , it investigates how each
modality affects user experience, usability , and effectiveness in supporting physical rehabilita-
tion for elderly users. The comparison takes into account both the technological affordances
and limitations of VR and AR, as well as how these impact the user’s ability to engage with
and benefit from the intervention.
Feasibility Study
Beyond comparison, the study assesses the feasibility of implementing such systems in a
real-world clinical or at-home context. This includes evaluating technical reliability , user
acceptance, safety , and the logistical aspects of deploying VR and AR applications for this
specific patient group. Considerations include setup complexity , interaction intuitiveness, and
adaptability to varied physical and cognitive abilities among the elderly population.
Target Group: Elderly Vestibular Patients
The intervention targets elderly individuals diagnosed with vestibular dysfunction, a con-
dition that affects balance and spatial orientation. These patients often require specialized
balance training, and the design of the programme must accommodate age and condition-
related challenges such as reduced mobility , sensory impairments and a potentially height-
ened sensitivity to cybersickness.
Exercise Type: Reactive Stepping
The focus is on reactive stepping exercises, a domain in the balance rehabilitation framework
STABLE. These exercises require users to quickly respond to unexpected prompts, simulating
real-world balance recovery situations. The project investigates how effectively VR and AR
environments can replicate these conditions and guide patients through them safely and
meaningfully .
Platform: Meta Quest 3
The study utilizes the Meta Quest 3, a standalone mixed reality headset capable of both VR
and AR functionalities. The choice of platform allows for a controlled comparison between
fully immersive (VR) and blended (AR) environments within the same hardware ecosystem,
ensuring consistent performance and interaction design constraints.
Clinical Collaboration and Time Frame

The programme was developed in collaboration with the Department of Physiology and Occu-
pational Therapy at Gentofte Hospital over a four-month period. This collaboration provided
access to clinical expertise, relevant test participants, and feedback from physiotherapists,
thereby grounding the project in a practical healthcare context and ensuring medical rele-

Evaluation Methods
The effectiveness of the VR and AR programmes was evaluated using a mixed-methods ap-
proach. This included a semi-structured interview to capture detailed, qualitative insights
into participants’ experiences and perceptions, and the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire
(SSQ) to collect quantitative data on cybersickness symptoms. This combination allowed for
both depth and consistency in the feedback, enabling us to explore user impressions while
also comparing responses across participants in a structured way .

3 Methodology and Reading Guide
This project adopts a structured, iterative methodology based on the Spiral Model, which
combines the strengths of both linear and agile development approaches. The process is di-
vided into four interconnected phases: Analysis, Design, Implementation, and Evaluation.
This model supports continuous refinement through feedback loops, enabling the team to
remain adaptive to user needs, clinical insights, and technological constraints throughout the
development cycle.
Analysis
The Analysis phase provides the theoretical foundation for the project. It begins with an ex-
ploration of the ageing population and the increasing prevalence of vestibular dysfunction,
focusing on the associated challenges of balance and mobility . The STABLE framework, devel-
oped by physiotherapist Kasper Søndergaard, is introduced as a clinically validated structure
for categorizing and addressing balance impairments. Within this framework, the domain

5.1.2 Balance and Ageing
Ageing is a complex process that affects multiple physiological systems, including those re-
sponsible for maintaining balance. As individuals grow older, they experience a decline in
sensory , motor and cognitive functions, all of which contribute to diminished postural stabil-
ity (Konrad et al., 1999). This decline increases the risk of falls, which are a major cause
of injury and reduced independence among older adults (J. A. Balogun, 1994). Understand-
ing the mechanisms behind balance deterioration with age is crucial for developing effective
interventions to enhance stability and prevent falls.
Postural control relies on the integration of multiple sensory inputs, including visual, vestibu-
lar and proprioceptive information. These inputs provide the central nervous system with
data on body positioning and movement, allowing for appropriate adjustments to maintain
equilibrium. However, with ageing, the efficiency of these systems declines. The vestibu-
lar system, which detects head movements and spatial orientation, undergoes degenerative
changes, including loss of hair cells and decreased nerve conduction velocity (Konrad et al.,
1999). Similarly , proprioceptive receptors in muscles and joints become less sensitive, im-
pairing the body’s ability to detect changes in position. The visual system also deteriorates,
with reduced contrast sensitivity and slower processing speeds, further compromising bal-
ance (J. A. Balogun, 1994).
In addition to sensory decline, musculoskeletal changes play a significant role in age-related
balance impairments. Sarcopenia, the loss of muscle mass and strength, affects postural con-
trol by reducing the ability to make rapid corrective movements. Joint stiffness and reduced
flexibility further hinder stability , making it more difficult for older adults to recover from
perturbations (Konrad et al., 1999). These physical changes are often compounded by neu-
rological factors, such as delayed reaction times and diminished motor coordination, leading
to a higher likelihood of falls.

Research has shown that balance performance peaks in early adulthood and gradually de-
clines with age. Studies using the single limb stance timed test indicate that balance stability
is highest in the third and fourth decades of life before progressively worsening (J. A. Balo-
gun, 1994). Men tend to perform better than women in balance assessments, possibly due to
differences in muscle mass and neuromuscular coordination. However, both sexes experience
a steady decline, with a marked deterioration after the age of 60.
Anthropometric factors, particularly body weight and height, have a measurable impact on
balance and postural control. Taller individuals generally possess a higher center of mass,
which increases the challenge of maintaining stability especially during dynamic movements
such as stepping. A higher center of mass amplifies postural sway and requires greater neu-
romuscular coordination to maintain equilibrium. This effect is particularly critical in elderly
individuals, who often experience diminished muscle strength, delayed reaction times and
impaired proprioception (Hue et al., 2007).
Similarly , increased body weight, particularly when concentrated in the upper body or ab-
dominal region, shifts the body’s center of gravity anteriorly and imposes greater mechanical
strain on the lower limbs and joints. This can impair the execution of rapid, corrective steps
and increase reliance on compensatory strategies that may not restore balance effectively
(Handrigan et al., 2010). Obesity has also been associated with poorer postural control,
slower walking speeds and a wider base of support, all of which are adaptations to reduced
stability (Greve et al., 2007).
On a macro social scale, the increased risk of falls and balance-related injuries due to anthro-
pometric factors contributes to higher healthcare costs and economic burden. Falls among
the elderly or individuals with obesity often lead to hospitalizations, long-term rehabilitation
and loss of independence, impacting families and community resources. These outcomes
place strain on healthcare systems and social support networks, emphasizing the need for
preventive strategies targeting at-risk populations.
Beyond physical limitations, difficulties with balance and mobility can affect mental health
and self-esteem. Individuals who struggle with maintaining posture may experience anxiety ,
fear of falling and decreased motivation to participate in social activities. This can lead
to social isolation and reduced overall well-being, highlighting the interconnectedness of
physical health and social integration.

Interventions aimed at mitigating balance decline focus on strengthening the neuromuscular
and sensory systems. Physical exercise, particularly strength and balance training, has been
shown to improve postural control and reduce fall risk. Activities such as Tai Chi, yoga and
resistance training enhance muscle strength, flexibility and coordination, thereby improving
balance (Konrad et al., 1999). Additionally , sensory training, including exercises that chal-
lenge proprioceptive and vestibular functions, can help compensate for age-related declines.
Gait training and fall prevention programs also play a crucial role in promoting stability and
confidence in older adults (J. A. Balogun, 1994).
Technological advancements are contributing to balance assessment and rehabilitation. Wear-

valuable data for personalised interventions. Virtual reality and biofeedback systems offer im-

risk in older individuals.
Given the growing ageing population, maintaining balance and preventing falls are critical
public health priorities. By understanding the physiological changes associated with ageing
and implementing targeted interventions, it is possible to enhance mobility , independence
and overall quality of life for older adults. A multidisciplinary approach that includes health-
care professionals, caregivers and community support is essential in fostering a safe and
active ageing process.

5.2 STABLE Domains
In the context of an ageing population, maintaining balance and preventing falls are critical
factors in sustaining autonomy and quality of life. Vestibular dysfunction, common among
older adults, poses a significant risk to postural stability , often resulting in a loss of confi-
dence in movement and increased fall incidents. To address these challenges, the STABLE
(Specific Training According to BaLance Evaluation) approach (Søndergaard, 2024) offers a
structured framework for balance assessment and targeted rehabilitation. The STABLE frame-
work was created by Søndergaard, a physiotherapist at Gentofte hospital. Søndergaard, who
we are collaborating with on this project, provided us with the task of developing an XR pro-
gramme focusing on rehabilitation within a single domain of our choosing. Understanding
these domains also provides valuable insight into designing effective interventions, whether
in Virtual Reality (VR) or Augmented Reality (AR) settings, which will be discussed in the
following sections.
The STABLE approach breaks balance ability into six distinct domains: stability limits, sen-
sory orientation, reactive stepping, power, turning and cognitive-motor interaction. These
domains correspond to essential components of balance and mobility , ensuring that interven-
tions target specific deficits rather than applying a one-size-fits-all method. The fundamental
premise of STABLE is that balance impairments must be addressed at their weakest point to
maximise overall improvement. This is analogous to reinforcing the weakest link in a chain,
neglecting the most impaired domain can limit the effectiveness of rehabilitation efforts. A
key advantage of the STABLE approach is its reliance on objective, quantitative measures
rather than subjective assessment scales. This enhances the reliability of evaluations and
ensures that training interventions are tailored precisely to the needs of each individual. Ad-
ditionally , STABLE provides a balance profile, visually representing the patient’s strengths
and weaknesses across the six domains, which facilitates the decision-making process for
healthcare professionals (Søndergaard, 2024).
Stability limits evaluate a person’s ability to extend their centre of mass (COM) relative to
their base of support (BOS) without stepping or falling. It is crucial for tasks that involve
reaching and leaning, making it directly relevant to stepping exercises. Sensory orientation
assesses the ability to integrate and interpret visual, proprioceptive and vestibular inputs
for maintaining postural control. Individuals with vestibular dysfunction often experience
34
PAGE 36

excessive reliance on one sensory modality , which can be targeted through training strategies
that challenge and enhance multisensory integration. (Søndergaard, 2024)
Power, particularly lower-limb power and the ability to generate force rapidly , plays a decisive
role in fall prevention. Age-related neuromuscular decline often affects fast-twitch muscle fi-
bres, reducing reaction time and strength. STABLE emphasises functional, high-velocity exer-
cises such as repeated sit-to-stand movements rather than isolated strength training. Turning
is another critical domain, as rapid and controlled turning is a fundamental aspect of mobil-
ity . Many falls occur during turning movements, particularly in environments with uneven
surfaces or unexpected obstacles. Training for this domain focuses on improving dynamic sta-
bility and coordinated movement. Reactive stepping, is crucial for recovering from instability
and preventing falls. This domain assesses a person’s ability to take quick, corrective steps
when balance is lost, an ability that can be significantly enhanced through targeted training.
Finally , cognitive-motor interaction is a crucial element, as balance control is not merely a
physical task but also involves cognitive processing. Walking while performing a secondary
cognitive task, such as responding to questions or navigating a complex environment, often
leads to an increased fall risk. Training interventions must incorporate cognitive challenges
alongside physical exercises to improve real-world applicability . (Søndergaard, 2024) On this
background we have made this a requirement for the implementation of our programme (for
the rest of the report, requirements being established will be noted like the following text:
see Requirements 6.3, this report, page 89, Exercises).
When considering which domain to prioritise for our programme, we undertook a detailed
analysis of their potential impact and feasibility . As other teams working within the STABLE
framework had already selected stability limits and sensory orientation, our focus shifted to
the remaining four domains. Cognitive-motor interaction was deemed less suitable following
discussions with a physiotherapist, as it involves a high degree of cognitive complexity that
may not align optimally with our intended intervention. This left power, turning and reactive
stepping. While power and turning are both crucial aspects of mobility , reactive stepping
emerged as the most pertinent domain due to its direct role in fall prevention. Falls often
occur when individuals fail to execute timely reactive steps, making this an essential area for
improvement. Moreover, reactive stepping can be effectively trained in controlled environ-
ments using VR-based perturbation scenarios, allowing for safe and progressive rehabilitation
(see Requirements 6.3, this report, page 89, Exercises) (Søndergaard, 2024).

5.3 Virtuality Continuum
The concept of the Virtuality Continuum (VC), was originally introduced by Milgram and
Kishino in 1994 and serves as a foundational framework for understanding the spectrum
between completely real and fully virtual environments (Milgram and Kishino, 1994). It
categorises immersive experiences based on their integration of real and virtual elements as
well as their ability to influence a user’s perception of reality . In the context of rehabilitation,
36
PAGE 38
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
understanding where various Extended Reality (XR) technologies fall on this continuum is
crucial for designing effective, user-friendly interventions.
Figure 12: An illustration of the Virtuality Continuum spectrum, which ranges from fully
real environments to fully virtual ones, including Augmented Reality (AR) and Augmented
Virtuality (AV) as stages in between (Interaction Design Foundation, n.d.)
As shown in Figure 12, the continuum spans from purely real environments, only consisting of
physical objects, to purely virtual environments, exclusively consisting of computer-generated
objects. Between these two extremes lies Mixed Reality (MR), which includes Augmented
Reality (AR), where real environments are augmented with virtual content and Augmented
Virtuality (AV), where virtual environments integrate real-world components.
Additionally , Milgram and Kishino, 1994 propose a taxonomy that classifies MR experiences
based on three dimensions: Extend of World Knowledge(EWK), Reproduction Fidelity (RF)
and Extent of Presence Metaphor (EPM). This taxonomy provides a framework for under-
standing and comparing developments across diverse fields, allowing a clearer evaluation of
the essential similarities and differences between various XR applications.
5.3.1 Extent of World Knowledge
The first of the three dimensions of the taxanomy is Extent of World Knowledge (EWK),
which represents how well the system understands the real-world environment, especially in
regards to identification and location of objects (Figure 13).
37
PAGE 39
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
Figure 13: The Extent of World Knowledge (EWK) axis representing the system’s understand-
ing of the real-world context (Milgram and Kishino, 1994)
Figure 13 illustrates the concept of EWK, which ranges from an unmodelled world to a com-
pletely modelled world. A system located on the left side of the spectrum has little to no
understanding of the real world environment, meaning that it is not able to recognise objects
or determine their locations. An example within this category is a 360-degree video using
Google Cardboard or a simple virtual reality experience that is entirely pre-programmed and
does not react to real-world inputs.
Moving towards the middle, World Partially Modelled, represents a system that can identify
"where" the objects are or "what" they are, but not necessarily both. For example, a mobile
AR application that uses plane detection to place virtual objects on flat surfaces, but cannot
identify or interact with real-world objects like furniture or people.
A system located on the right end of the spectrum has full knowledge of its surroundings and
thereby allows for highly accurate interactions between virtual and real elements (Milgram
and Kishino, 1994). For instance, Microsoft HoloLens enables mixed reality applications
where digital content dynamically responds to real-world geometry and objects because of
its spatial mapping and object recognition capabilities.
In the context of rehabilitation, the Extent of World Knowledge may have an impact on how
effectively an XR system can support physio-therapeutic interventions. While a high EWK
expands the range of a system’s abilities and thereby enhances the interactive experience, it
may not always be necessary , depending on the intended use case.
A low EWK system can be useful for passive rehabilitation exercises or guided visualisation
where real-time interactions are not required. Such systems can be used to deliver pre-
programmed content without tracking or responding to user movements.
In contrast, when rehabilitation tasks need to involve a more complex interaction between
the virtual and physical environments, a higher EWK becomes beneficial. In these scenarios,

the system’s ability to accurately map and recognise objects enhances the level of interaction
as well as the relevance of the provided feedback. When applied to vestibular rehabilitation,
this can especially be used to apply visual and auditory cues to user interactions in real time
and properly assist them throughout the activity .
Based on the above, choosing the appropriate level of EWK should take into account the
specific requirements of the rehabilitation intervention. Since the system for this project is
intended to track user movements and allow interaction with virtual objects, a moderate to
high EWK is necessary to provide precise tracking and responsive feedback (See Requirements
6, this report, page 89, System). This will allow to dynamically follow user’s movements and
adapt to their changes.

5.3.4 Reevaluating the Virtuality Continuum
While definition of Virtuality Continuum by (Milgram and Kishino, 1994) remains a funda-
mental framework in understanding the spectrum between real and virtual environments,
recent advancements in technology and research suggest that certain aspects of their original
taxonomy require reevaluation. The framework, initially developed in 1994, was primarily
focused on visual displays and hardware capabilities available at that time. However, modern
developments in Extended Reality (XR) have introduced new considerations that challenge
the original model’s completeness and applicability .
In the article "Revisiting Milgram and Kishino’s Reality-Virtuality Continuum" by (Skarbez et
al., 2021), the authors take a fresh look at the framework and suggest necessary revisions
to account for advancements in immersive technology . One of the key critiques here is that
the Virtuality Continuum is not truly continuous. (Milgram and Kishino, 1994) describe an
endpoint of fully virtual environments, while (Skarbez et al., 2021) argue that this idea of a
truly "pure" virtual experience is unattainable, as users’ bodily awareness remains anchored
in reality . Even in highly immersive virtual environments, users still retain sensory input
from their real-world surroundings, such as vestibular and proprioceptive feedback. They
propose that instead of a seamless progression from real to virtual, the continuum contains a
discontinuity , where conventional virtual reality remains inherently mixed due to its reliance
on real-world sensory input.
Another issue is the continuum’s exclusive focus on visual displays. While this was relevant
in the 1990s, modern XR experiences engage multiple senses, including haptic feedback and
spatialised audio. By overlooking multimodal interactions, the original taxonomy fails to fully
capture modern XR experiences. For that reason, Skarbez et al., 2021 argue that an updated
41
PAGE 43
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
framework must reflect how multiple sensory modalities shape immersion.
To address these gaps, Skarbez et al., 2021 introduce a revisited taxonomy with the three
following dimensions:
1. Extent of World Knowledge (EWK): adopted directly from the original taxonomy
2. Immersion (IM): a combination of Reproduction Fidelity and Extent of Presence Metaphor,
tackling the similarity between the two and taking multisensory interaction into account
3. Coherence (CO): how consistently the system conveys its intention to the user and how
well sensory inputs align to create a seamless experience
This taxonomy shifts the focus from hardware to user experience, emphasising the impor-
tance of multimodality and sensory alignment for effective XR applications (see Requirements
6.2, this report, page 87, Feedback). The updates are particularly relevant for XR-based re-
habilitation, where real-time adaptation and precise movement tracking are essential. High
EWK ensures accurate monitoring, while strong CO aligns visual, auditory and haptic feed-
back to support therapy . Recognising discontinuities in the continuum, expanding MR’s def-
inition and incorporating multimodal interactions provide a more accurate framework for
modern XR applications, especially in rehabilitation, where user perception and sensory co-
herence are essential.
5.3.5 Summary: Virtuality Continuum
• The Virtuality Continuum highlights the spectrum from real to virtual environments, but
modern XR systems rely on multiple sensory inputs, not just visuals. This is particularly
important in rehabilitation, where balance between immersion, user awareness and
sensory feedback is crucial.
• The continuum includes three classification dimensions—Extent of World Knowledge
(EWK), Reproduction Fidelity (RF), and Extent of Presence Metaphor (EPM) that assess
a system’s real-world awareness, realism, and user immersion.
• Higher EWK and EPM are especially valuable in rehabilitation contexts like vestibular
therapy , enabling real-time, responsive environments that support active user engage-
ment and tailored feedback.

5.4 The use of XR Production Within Rehabilitation
The use of Virtual Reality (VR) in vestibular rehabilitation has gained increasing attention
due to its potential to enhance traditional therapeutic approaches. Vestibular disorders, often
associated with dizziness, balance impairments and visual instability , pose significant chal-
lenges to affected individuals. Traditional vestibular rehabilitation typically involves physical
therapy techniques such as gaze stabilisation and postural control exercises (see requirements
6, this report, page 87, Exercises). However, VR-based interventions offer a modern, immer-
sive approach that may improve patient engagement and therapeutic outcomes. This section
will examine existing studies evaluating the use of Virtual Reality as a tool in vestibular reha-
bilitation in order to gather insights on its advantages and limitations.
In the article by (Hazzaa et al., 2023), a meta-analysis was conducted to assess the effec-
tiveness of VR-based programs in vestibular rehabilitation. The research process initially
screened 501 articles, narrowing the selection to six controlled trials that compared VR re-
habilitation with conventional vestibular therapy and medical treatments. The findings re-
vealed that VR improved key outcome measures such as the Dizziness Handicap Inventory
(DHI), Vertigo Symptom Scale-Short Form (VSS-SF) and Activities-specific Balance Confi-
dence (ABC) scale. Additionally , no study reported major side effects, significant incidents or
fall following the use of virtual reality .
Similarly , (Bergeron et al., 2015) conducted a systematic review to evaluate the role of VR
in the treatment of vestibular disorders. The review found that VR-based rehabilitation led
to a mean decrease of 27 points on the DHI scale, shifting patients from moderate to mild
handicap categories. Moreover, the study highlighted the role of exposure duration, detect-
ing a correlation between the total time spent in VR therapy and the extent of therapeutic
benefits. Based on these findings, the authors recommended a minimum of 120-150 minutes
of cumulative VR exposure to achieve optimal rehabilitation outcomes.
(Alahmari et al., 2013) compared Virtual Reality-based therapy with customised vestibu-
lar physical therapy in a clinical trial. The study involved participants engaging in either
43
PAGE 45
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
VR-based treadmill walking in a virtual grocery store environment or traditional gaze sta-
bilisation and balance exercises. Both groups demonstrated improvements in dizziness and
balance, with no major differences in outcomes. However, the VR group reported higher
engagement and satisfaction, suggesting that immersive therapy may enhance patient adher-
ence to rehabilitation programs. It should also be noted that the setup for the VR did not
include a head-mounted display , but instead a three-projector configuration, making it less
representative of the intended use case for this project.
Despite these promising results, VR-based vestibular rehabilitation also has its limitations.
Some of the concerns mentioned by (Hazzaa et al., 2023) include system latency , which
can influence the patient’s experience. The delay between capturing user input and provid-
ing multisensory feedback (latency), may be noticeable to patients, potentially altering their
natural responses. The article states that this discrepancy can lead to differences in depth
perception and distance estimation, making VR experiences less representative of real-world
scenarios.
Another challenge discussed by both (Hazzaa et al., 2023) and (Bergeron et al., 2015) is the
occurrence of cybersickness connected to unnatural and sometimes conflicting multisensory
stimuli in VR experiences (see Requirements 6.3, this report, page 89, System). However,
both articles also state that these symptoms tend to diminish over time as patients habituate
to VR environments.
In comparison to VR, the research on the use of Augmented Reality (AR) or Mixed Reality
(MR) in vestibular rehabilitation remains limited, proposing the need for further investigation
of the area (see Requirements 6.3, this report, page 89, System). Understanding how AR and
MR can be effectively integrated into vestibular rehabilitation could expand the range of XR-
based therapeutic approaches and provide new opportunities for patient-centred care. The
choice of the most suitable EX technology for this project should be closely linked to elderly
individuals as the target audience, which may require a certain approach in order for them
to be assisted in the best possible way during the experience.
In conclusion, the findings from existing studies highlight both the potential benefits and
challenges of implementing VR-based vestibular rehabilitation. They present promising re-
sults in improving balance, reducing dizziness symptoms and enhancing patient engagement
but also indicate that careful consideration must be given to optimising VR system design to

reduce latency and implementing strategies to minimise cybersickness. Additionally , the lim-

5.4.1 Elderly’s Acceptance of Extended Reality
The adoption of XR among elderly individuals is influenced by a range of factors, including
ease of use, perceived benefits and concerns related to discomfort or cybersickness. Research
indicates that XR technologies, particularly AR and VR, have the potential to enhance reha-

therapy regimens (Blomqvist et al., 2021). However, the effectiveness of these technologies
relies on their ability to be seamlessly integrated into the daily lives of elderly users, with
minimal barriers to adoption.
One of the key benefits of XR in rehabilitation is its ability to increase motivation through
interactive and gamified experiences. Kappen et al., 2020 highlights that gamification de-

hering to this can help maintain sustained engagement in physical activity by aligning with
their motivational preferences.
Studies also show that older adults often struggle with adherence to traditional rehabilitation
exercises, especially when performed at home without supervision (Blomqvist et al., 2021).
AR and VR provide an immersive environment where users receive real-time feedback on
their progress, reinforcing positive behaviors and encouraging consistent participation. In
a feasibility study using the Microsoft HoloLens for balance training, participants (7, 66-
88 years) reported that the technology made the exercises more engaging and enjoyable,
leading to higher motivation levels (Blomqvist et al., 2021). Moreover, the interactive nature
of XR can stimulate cognitive functions, potentially offering dual benefits in both physical
and mental health.

5.7 Sense of Presence and Cybersickness
The concept of presence is a critical consideration in the development of VR applications and
virtual environments in general. Although HMDs only became commercially accessible in
the 2010s, VR technology has existed for approximately six decades, since Ivan Sutherland
created the first AR and VR HMD (Dede, 2009),(Slater, 2018). Consequently , extensive
research has been conducted over the years, leading to a well-established understanding of
concepts such as presence and immersion.
Schuemie et al., 2001 conducted a comprehensive literature review on presence in VR,
putting forth various definitions and theoretical perspectives proposed by multiple researchers.
A general consensus exists that presence can be characterised as the subjective experience of
"being there" within a virtual environment. While the desirability of presence is widely ac-
knowledged, its precise effects and implications remain a subject of ongoing debate (Schuemie
et al., 2001). Moreover, terminological variations persist across different theoretical frame-
works, with terms such as immersion, presence and engagement being used interchangeably
58
PAGE 60
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
through the years (Nilsson et al., 2016). Given this, literature aiding the development of our
VR application will be judged by its relevance to the project and its definition of the term,
whether they describe it as immersion, presence or something else.
Nilsson et al., 2016 created a three-dimensional taxonomy of various conceptualisations of
immersion, dividing existing definitions into three categories, representing a dimension of
the taxonomy (Figure 20). They are as follows; immersion as a property of a system, a sub-
jective response to narrative contents, or a subjective response to challenges within the virtual
environment.

This taxonomy can be used to analyse existing mediated experiences (or unmediated, 0.0.0
in the model) or can help us when creating a new experience. The system immersion of
our application will be mostly handled by the qualities of the chosen HMD (Meta Quets 3).
Nilsson et al., 2016, in line with Slater, 2003, describe system immersion as an objectively
measurable property of the system and not the product of the user’s reaction to being enveloped
by technology. This leaves us at (0.1.0) on figure 20. System immersion alone hardly produces

an immersive mediated experience. In the context of this project, it makes the most sense to
pursue the challenge-based immersion dimension. Quoting Ermi and Mäyrä, 2005, Nilsson
et al., 2016 describe it as the feeling of immersion that is at its most powerful when one is able
to achieve a satisfying balance of challenges and abilities . They go on to discuss challenges as
either sensorimotor or mental skills and that both will most likely be present in some capacity .
To achieve immersion in the shape of the aforementioned ’balance’, they argue that one of the
two following conditions should be true; Either the simultaneous occurrence of the challenges
has to be brief enough to avoid attentional overload, or the user needs to be so proficient at

second one. In our case, the important focus must lie on the sensorimotor challenges, given
the context of the product. As implied, mental challenges can still be present but should be
simple enough to be automated, so the user can focus on the sensorimotor challenges.

5.8 Gamified vs. Non-Gamified
While cybersickness is an important consideration when developing VR applications, particu-
larly for older adults, the absence of significant findings in this age group opens opportunities
to explore various design approaches more freely . One such design decision involves the use
of gamification. As the focus shifts from minimising discomfort to maximising engagement
and adherence, it becomes essential to evaluate whether a gamified or non-gamified approach
better supports rehabilitation goals for elderly users with vestibular dysfunction.
When designing a rehabilitation programme, a critical decision is whether to incorporate
gamification. Gamified approaches integrate game-like elements such as points, levels, re-
wards and challenges to enhance engagement and motivation. Non-gamified approaches,
by contrast, maintain a more traditional, structured format focused purely on therapeutic
exercises without additional game mechanics. Both approaches have their merits and draw-
backs, particularly when applied to rehabilitation for elderly individuals with balance diffi-
culties.
Gamification has been widely acknowledged as a means to increase engagement, motivation
and adherence to rehabilitation programmes. Research suggests that incorporating game me-
chanics can enhance user experience and encourage sustained participation, which is crucial
for effective rehabilitation (Proffitt and Lange, 2015). For older adults, rehabilitation can
62
PAGE 64
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
often be repetitive and demotivating, leading to decreased compliance. By adding interac-
tive and rewarding elements, gamification may help counteract these issues (Lewis et al.,
2016).
Another advantage of gamification is that it can introduce adaptive difficulty and feedback
allowing exercises to be progressively challenging while maintaining user interest. Virtual
Reality (VR) or Augmented Reality (AR) gamified interventions can provide real-time feed-
back and simulate real-world scenarios, which are essential for training balance and reactive
stepping in a controlled environment (Lohse et al., 2013). These systems can also promote
cognitive engagement by requiring users to make quick decisions, thus reinforcing both motor
and cognitive functions.
A study by Gandolfi et al. (Layth Khaleel et al., 2016) investigated how specific gamifica-
tion elements influenced engagement and motivation in a VR-based shoulder rehabilitation
context. The researchers implemented features such as scoreboards, levels, timers and vi-
sual/audio feedback within their VR games. Their findings showed that these gamified com-
ponents significantly improved users’ perception of fun, immersion and challenge, factors
that contributed to stronger engagement and willingness to continue the rehabilitation ex-
ercises. Notably , participants reported that the inclusion of a timer and visual and audio
feedback enhanced focus and competitiveness, while progressive levels and score tracking
provided a clear sense of achievement. These insights suggest that even simple gamification
strategies can increase motivation and adherence, two aspects highly relevant for designing
rehabilitation programmes.
However, a major limitation of gamification is that it can sometimes prioritise entertainment
over effectiveness. If not carefully designed, game elements may distract from the core re-
habilitation objectives, leading to decreased efficacy . Additionally , for some elderly users,
overly complex game interfaces can be intimidating or frustrating, potentially reducing par-
ticipation.
Non-gamified rehabilitation approaches, while less engaging for some, offer a direct, clini-
cally focused method of therapy . Traditional physiotherapy exercises have been extensively
validated and are often easier to standardise and control for research and clinical application
(Shubert, 2011). Without the added cognitive load of a game environment, patients may
be able to focus more on the precise execution of movements, ensuring optimal therapeutic
63
PAGE 65
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
benefit.
For elderly populations, particularly those with cognitive decline or limited prior experience
with digital technology , a non-gamified approach can be more accessible. Straightforward,
familiar exercises may reduce technology-induced anxiety and ensure that patients are not
discouraged from participating. Additionally , non-gamified methods allow physiotherapists
to tailor interventions in real time based on patient feedback without the constraints imposed
by pre-programmed game mechanics.
On the downside, non-gamified approaches risk lower engagement and motivation, which are
key factors in adherence to long-term rehabilitation. If exercises feel monotonous, patients
may not commit fully to their rehabilitation plan, reducing overall effectiveness.
Both gamified and non-gamified approaches have their respective strengths and limitations
and their suitability depends on the specific needs of the patient group. Gamification offers
a compelling method to boost engagement and adherence, particularly for those who may
struggle with motivation. However, it requires careful design to ensure that game elements
do not overshadow therapeutic goals. Conversely , non-gamified methods provide a clinically
robust and straightforward approach, better suited for patients who prefer structure or who
may struggle with interactive digital platforms.
Given that engagement is a critical factor in rehabilitation success, we have chosen to adopt
a hybrid approach that incorporates light gamified elements while prioritising clinical effec-
tiveness. Rather than fully gamifying the experience, we selectively integrate features such
as progress tracking, adaptive feedback and a dynamic, interactive environment. These ele-
ments are designed to enhance motivation and user engagement without overcomplicating
the interface or undermining the therapeutic focus. In doing so, we aim to maximise both
patient adherence and the overall effectiveness of the rehabilitation programme.

5.9.1 UX Laws
UX laws are grounded in cognitive psychology and aim to explain how users perceive, interact
with and process digital interfaces. Applying these principles helps improve usability , reduce
friction and support user motivation. This is particularly important in rehabilitation contexts,
where the interaction should feel intuitive and encourage engagement. In the following, we
highlight selected UX laws that were most relevant to our application’s design decisions.
Choice Overload:
The ’choice overload’ UX law refers to how the amount of choices that you provide the user
with can negatively affect their outcome. Too many choices can overwhelm the user and
leave them in a state of "analysis paralysis". This state happens when there is too much
information for the user to be able to comprehend, which then leads to them being unable
to make a decision, due to the fear that any decision feels like the incorrect one. Although
options and choices can be beneficial, too many can distress and discourage the user, leading
to the behavioural science principle of choice overload. (Clinehens, 2019)
In the book "The Paradox of Choice", the author suggests steps to follow when decision mak-
ing. The following are those steps:
1. Figure out your goals, Evaluate the importance of each goal
2. Array the options according to how well they meet each goal
3. Evaluate how likely each of the options is to meet your goals
4. Pick the winning option (Clinehens, 2019)
As stated by William James “ The art of being wise is the art of knowing what to overlook. ”
(Clinehens, 2019), the designers must know which elements are less relevant for the user
and remove the option for them, in order to not overload them with choices. Therefore when
designing our programme, we must keep the choices for the users to the minimum and only
include the most relevant options, using the Paradox of Choice steps (see Requirements 6.2,
this report, page 87, UI).

Jakob’s Law:
Jakob’s Law is a simple concept, that states that users prefer a new product to look and
function similarly to something they already are familiar with. Therefore, to encourage good
user experience the design must be familiar to the user, allowing them to transfer knowledge
and expectations from other products that they have previously used. This will result in
streamlining their learning curve, allowing the user to effortlessly navigate and interact with
the product. (Csincsak, 2023a)
In relation to our programme, to create a better user experience that resonates with the
user, we must observe some of the exercises that the patients conduct during a physiotherapy
session and transfer/align the mental model (a person’s internal understanding or represen-
tation of how something works in the real world, which helps them predict outcomes, make
decisions and interact with systems or environments effectively) to our programme (see Re-
quirements 6.3, this report, page 89, Exercises).
Law of Common Region:
Law of common region is a Gestalt principle and is a tool used to create a user-centred
interface. Simply put, it posits that elements that are surrounded by a border, are perceived
as a group. Therefore, when designing elements that purposefully must be grouped together,
a border is an effective way to visually signal to the user that the elements belong together.
(Csincsak, 2023b)
In our case, law of common region does not apply to the user interface or menu, but rather
functions as a principle of spatial grouping within the VR environment. The interactive space
must be framed by the edges of the virtual lake, which serves as a natural boundary , applying
the Law of Common Region (see Requirements 6.3, this report, page 89, Environment /
Setting). This helps users intuitively understand which elements (e.g., lily pads, frog) are
part of the active exercise area and which are purely environmental. Such spatial grouping
reinforces clarity and helps users focus on the task without explicit instructions.
Law of Similarity:
Law of similarity is another Gestalt principle. This principle suggests that elements with sim-
ilar traits and visual characteristics are often grouped together by the user. Law of similarity
and law of common region are some of the original set of visual grouping principles. The ele-
66
PAGE 68
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
ments do not have to be identical to each other, but simply share some visual characteristics,
such as colour, shape or size, to be perceived as a group. (Harley, 2020)
In our programme, this principle will not be applied to user interface or menu design, but
is instead used to visually group interactive elements in the environment, specifically the lily
pads. By giving lily pads of the same difficulty level similar size and colours, users intu-
itively recognise patterns and associate these elements with each other (see Requirements
6.3, this report, page 89, Environment / Setting). This visual consistency reduces cognitive
load and supports quick decision-making, especially for elderly users who may benefit from
clear, repeated visual cues.
Paradox of the Active User:
The ’paradox of the active user’ is a very relevant UX law, for when active users are introduced
to a new programme, mainly during the onboarding process. Active users refer to those
who jump straight into using a system without reading the manual or tutorials, focus on
completing their immediate task rather than learning the system thoroughly and are typically
motivated by achieving a goal, not by exploring how the system works. This theory claims
that active users rarely read through the traditional user tours and that they are seen as more
of a hindrance towards them completing their immediate task, even if it increases the risk
of errors. Therefore, to meet the needs of active users, designers can make the guidance
or tutorial of how a programme works, blend in with the overall context of use. This can be
done in many ways, for example via tooltips which are lightweight information segments that
subtly appear at a relevant time, or via progressive onboarding which gradually introduces
the user to the system and allows them to discover additional functionalities as they progress.
(Yablonski, 2024)
In our programme it would therefore be ideal to not follow the traditional route of onboard-
ing, but rather blend the tutorial in to the actual context of use. This could be done by
incorporating a ghost demonstration that guides the user through how they navigate through
the programme (see Requirements 6.3, this report, page 89, Exercises).
While numerous other UX laws and psychological principles could be applied in the design of
digital systems, we have chosen to focus on those most relevant to our rehabilitation context
and target user group. The selected laws were prioritised due to their direct applicability
in designing an interface that is intuitive, familiar and non-overwhelming for elderly users

undergoing physiotherapy . By grounding our design choices in these core principles, we
aimed to reduce cognitive load, improve usability and support user engagement in a way
that aligns with both clinical goals and user expectations.
5.9.2 Mise-en-Scène Elements
In visual storytelling and immersive design, the term mise-en-scène refers to the arrangement
of everything that appears in the frame; setting, lighting, props, costume and staging. In the
context of our VR balance rehabilitation programme, these elements are crucial not for narra-
tive purposes, but for creating a supportive, focused and believable therapeutic environment.
Each aspect of the mise-en-scène contributes to user engagement, presence and ease of use,
factors that are especially important for older adults participating in balance training.
Lighting plays a critical role in shaping the overall immersive experience and is particularly
important in a rehabilitation context, where clarity , comfort and spatial awareness are essen-
tial. In our VR programme, careful consideration must be given to lighting design to support
both usability and user well-being.
Poor lighting, such as dim or unevenly lit environments, can make it difficult for users to
perceive important visual elements like virtual guides, floor boundaries or representations of
their own body . This is especially problematic in balance training, where visual cues help pa-
tients align their posture, monitor limb positioning and assess movement accuracy . If shadows
obscure critical parts of the environment or the user’s avatar, the patient may misinterpret
tasks or develop incorrect movement patterns, potentially diminishing the effectiveness of
the therapy .
Conversely , overcompensating by using overly bright or high-contrast lighting introduces a
different set of challenges. Harsh lighting can lead to sensory overload, discomfort, or even
cybersickness. Excessively bright environments can feel clinical or impersonal, detracting
from the calming and supportive atmosphere necessary for focused rehabilitation. Further-
more, high lighting contrast may unintentionally draw attention to irrelevant environmental
details, creating visual noise that distracts from task-relevant interactions. This refers not
only to the virtual environment, but also to the physical surroundings.
Good lighting is essential for both VR and AR programmes to function optimally , especially
since the Quest 3 uses external cameras for tracking (see Requirements 6.2, this report, page
68

87, Exercises). Poor lighting in the real world can interfere with accurate tracking, introduc-
ing technical disruptions that reduce immersion and usability . Therefore, lighting in our ap-
plication is designed to maintain a neutral and natural ambience, avoiding extreme contrasts
while ensuring sufficient visibility of interactive elements. Soft, diffuse lighting supports a
comfortable and safe virtual space, fostering both immersion and functional performance.
These choices enhance user confidence and reduce potential disorientation during stepping
tasks, ultimately contributing to a more effective balance training experience.
Setting is another essential element of mise-en-scène when developing an immersive VR ex-
perience. The setting refers to the environment and the set design, including the objects and
props, that are created for a scene. Props and objects are commonly conflated assuming that
they refer to the same, although they serve different purposes and functionalities. Passive
or background objects in a scene may help build realism or immersion, but are not essential
to the user’s task or interaction. Props, on the other hand, are objects that have a specific
purpose within a scene. Props have purpose and are there to be noticed, used or interacted
with. Our application must contain a prop that helps guide the user (see Requirements 6.3,
this report, page 89, Exercises). The setting is designed to convey different scenarios and can
be created using different techniques. The different techniques are artificial, which is when a
studio is used, real, which is on location, or digital, which is when CGI is used.
The setting of the VR environment should be calming, immersive and relevant to the thera-
peutic goals of the application (see Requirements 6.2, this report, page 87, Exercises). For
our balance programme, we consider a nature-inspired scene, such as a stylised pond or for-
est clearing, to promote a sense of calm and reduce cognitive load. Natural environments
have been shown to support relaxation, which may be beneficial in reducing anxiety or stress
during balance exercises, particularly in elderly users.
It is important that the setting avoids unnecessary visual clutter while still providing enough
environmental context to support spatial orientation. Elements like gentle terrain variation,
subtle vegetation and ambient natural sounds could enhance presence without overwhelming
the user. Consideration must also be given to system performance, particularly on standalone
devices like the Meta Quest 3. Overly complex environments could degrade performance
and impact comfort. Therefore, simplicity , clarity and relevance should guide environmental
design decisions.

Costume and makeup is also another key aspect within mise-en-scène, as they can help create
different experiences. Costume and makeup are commonly used to signify personality , pass-
ing of time, different realities, character progression, authenticity and historical accuracy .

as functional components that shape the user’s perception, motivation and ability to perform
tasks. By analysing these elements early in the design process, we can ensure that our appli-
cation aligns with user needs, supports immersion and ultimately enhances the rehabilitation
experience.

5.9.3 Audio and Visuals
In immersive applications such as VR rehabilitation, audio and visual design are not just
aesthetic enhancements, they play a central role in guiding user behaviour, providing feed-
back and creating an engaging and comprehensible experience. For elderly users undergoing
balance training, these sensory elements must be deliberately designed to support clarity ,
comfort and motivation while avoiding cognitive overload.
The use of sound in the balance VR programme is purposeful. Each sound effect is designed
to reinforce user actions and guide attention without being intrusive or distracting.
When a lily pad appears, a soft bubbly sound mimics the sensation of it rising from the water.
This auditory cue signals the start of a task or stepping opportunity and helps draw the user’s
attention to the new target. Conversely , when a lily pad disappears, a sound resembling a
splash provides confirmation that the interaction is complete. These water-themed sounds are
not only thematically consistent with the natural setting but also serve as intuitive auditory
feedback that supports action and timing.
Additionally , the frog emits ambient frog-like noises at intervals. This serves a dual purpose:
reinforcing the setting as a tranquil, natural environment and signalling the frog’s presence as
a guiding figure. While the frog sounds are primarily atmospheric, they can also be used con-
textually to draw the user’s attention, such as when transitioning between stages or guiding
direction.
Importantly , all audio elements must be designed with accessibility and comfort in mind.
Sudden, loud, or high-pitched sounds should be avoided to prevent discomfort or startle
responses, especially in an older population. Sounds should remain soft, natural and spaced
out, complementing the visual feedback rather than competing with it.
Visual elements form the primary interface between the user and the task, making their clarity
and communicative power vital to the rehabilitation experience. Our VR programme makes
use of simple yet distinct visual cues to ensure ease of understanding and reduce cognitive
friction.
The coloured lily pads are a key visual element, with different colours representing differ-
ent difficulty levels. This colour-coding helps users anticipate task demands and distinguish
between exercise types without requiring verbal explanation. The lily pads also appear and

5.9.4 Meaning in Media
In immersive digital experiences, meaning is not only embedded in content but constructed
through interaction, setting and sensory design. Within our balance-focused VR rehabili-

participates in shaping the user’s emotional and cognitive experience. Drawing from media
theory , we can identify different layers of meaning that inform and influence how users make
sense of what they encounter: referential, explicit, implicit and symptomatic meaning.

Referential meaning is grounded in what the media directly points to in the real world; the
recognisable, surface-level context. In our case, the virtual pond, lily pads and naturalistic
audio cues reference familiar elements from the physical world. These references anchor the
user in an environment that is comprehensible and non-threatening, which is particularly
important for elderly users who may be unfamiliar with VR. The setting suggests calmness,
fluidity and nature, all of which tie symbolically to the concept of balance and healing. This
makes the experience feel more relatable, helping users situate their actions within a mean-
ingful framework even without explicit explanation.
Explicit meaning emerges from the immediate, literal function of the programme: to guide
users through balance exercises in a supportive and engaging environment. Everything in the
system, from the colour-coded lily pads to the guiding frog, serves a direct and communicative
purpose. The user knows what to do, when to do it and receives clear feedback on their
performance. These surface meanings are reinforced by consistent visual and auditory cues
that support task clarity .
Implicit meaning, on the other hand, is more interpretive and emotional. It arises through
the relationships between design elements, interactions and the user’s own experiences. For
instance, the act of stepping onto lily pads may be interpreted as a form of progression or
growth. The non-verbal guidance offered by the frog might be seen as supportive compan-
ionship, adding a layer of empathy or care to the task structure. These interpretations are not
dictated by the system but enabled by it, allowing the user to derive personal meaning from
their experience over time.
Symptomatic meaning refers to the broader cultural or ideological context that the media
unintentionally reveals. In this case, the application reflects current societal values around
holistic healthcare, patient empowerment and the blending of clinical effectiveness with user-
centred design. By rejecting hyper-gamified or clinical aesthetics in favour of a more natu-
ralistic and calming approach, the programme subtly communicates a belief in the value of
well-being beyond metrics alone, acknowledging that recovery is not just mechanical, but
emotional and cognitive as well.
By considering these layers of meaning during the design process, we aim to create an ex-
perience that resonates with users beyond its functional purpose. The goal is not to impose
narrative, but to foster conditions in which users can find coherence, comfort and even a

subtle sense of narrative in their rehabilitation journey . Meaning is constructed through do-

5.9.5 Perception in Design
Perception plays a pivotal role in the design of immersive virtual environments, particularly
when the target users are older adults with vestibular impairments. Understanding how
users perceive space, motion and visual cues can significantly influence both usability and
effectiveness of the application. In our programme, we made deliberate design decisions that
take into account the perceptual abilities and limitations of our target group.
One key aspect involves the use of depth cues and motion clarity . Since the application re-
quires patients to react to and step toward appearing lily pads, the system relies on perceptual
accuracy to judge distances and directions. To support this, we will use visual elements such
as ground shadows and scale consistency , which help users interpret spatial relationships in
the environment.
Moreover, the perception of time and motion is relevant in the stepping tasks. We must
avoid overly quick animations or abrupt transitions, as these could disorient users or even
exacerbate balance issues. Instead, temporal cues, like the soft appearance of lily pads, help
guide action without inducing stress or confusion.
From a perceptual psychology standpoint, figure-ground distinction and visual salience have
also been considered. For instance, contrasting the lily pads against the water background
aids in quick target identification. Colour coding difficulty levels further enhances perceptual
grouping, ensuring the user understands which targets to prioritise.
An additional and critical perceptual dimension in balance rehabilitation is proprioception;
the body’s ability to sense its own position, movement and orientation in space (Foundation,
2025). While VR cannot replicate proprioceptive feedback in the same way as the real world,

visual and auditory cues can be used to support it. In our system, visual representation
of the user’s feet and shadow helps reinforce correct body alignment. The repeated action

5.9.6 Input and Output Technologies
Effective interaction within a VR system hinges on the technologies that mediate input and
output. These channels determine how users engage with the application and how informa-
tion is communicated back to them. Therefore, we have to make conscious decisions about
how both input and output systems should function to align with the physical and cognitive
needs of older users.
Input is primarily handled through the Meta Quest 3’s controller tracking and head tracking
features. The system detects head orientation and positional movement to trigger the step-
ping tasks. Importantly , we will attempt to avoid the use of traditional buttons or complex
handheld controllers, as these could pose usability challenges or create unnecessary barriers
for a population not familiar with gaming devices. We will also avoid the use of hand track-
ing, since it could potentially interrupt the controller tracking, as well as potentially confusing
the elderly patients, since it is a new concept to many of them.
The patient’s feet will be tracked using the controllers or an external sensor, to ensure ac-
curate physical alignment. Future versions of the system may explore more advanced input
options, such as pressure mats or foot sensors, to improve responsiveness.
Output spans across visual, auditory and haptic modalities. Visually , the system will provide
coloured lily pads when choosing the difficulty level, ghost demonstrations and an animated
frog to guide the user. Auditorily , ambient frog sounds and event-triggered effects (such as

5.9.7 Micro and Macro Design
In interaction and experience design, it is useful to distinguish between micro and macro
levels of design. This framework allows us to address both the fine-grained details that shape
moment-to-moment interactions and the larger structure that gives coherence and purpose
to the overall experience. In the context of our VR balance rehabilitation programme, both
perspectives have informed key design decisions.
Micro design refers to the small-scale, immediate interactions that occur between the user
and the system. These include how stepping targets appear and disappear, how feedback
is delivered and how transitions are handled between actions. In our application, we must
pay particular attention to the clarity and timing of such interactions. For example, the slight
delay and soft animation when a lily pad rises from the water, combined with a subtle “splash”
sound, makes the interaction feel responsive yet non-jarring.
Other micro-level elements include visual feedback provided by the coloured lily pads, the
fading in of ghost shoes during onboarding and the splash sound as a lily pad disappears.
These features reinforce successful actions, support intuitive understanding and help the user
maintain focus without becoming overwhelmed. Attention to micro-level details is especially
important in rehabilitation, where precision, comfort and consistency can significantly impact
user confidence and motor performance.
Macro design addresses the overall structure, flow and narrative logic of the experience.
This includes the sequence of tasks, the rhythm of the session, the use of guidance and
progression and how different components connect into a coherent whole. In our case, the

macro structure is intentionally simple and repeatable, supporting the needs of elderly users
who may benefit from predictability and routine.
The user begins with a gentle onboarding sequence guided by ghost shoes and subtle frog
gestures, which sets the tone and introduces the mechanics. From there, exercises progress
through clear, rhythmic stepping sequences that align with difficulty tiers. The nature-based
environment, auditory ambience and minimal interface all contribute to a macro-level expe-
rience that is calming and focused, reinforcing the therapeutic goals of balance, control and
mental presence.
Additionally , macro design considerations include how user feedback is structured over time.
Rather than using overt scoring systems or competitive game mechanics, the application pro-
vides implicit feedback through progress in difficulty levels and increasingly complex stepping
patterns. This supports engagement without introducing unnecessary pressure or competi-
tion, maintaining the clinical relevance of the experience.
Effective design occurs when the micro and macro levels reinforce each other. In our system,
small interactions (micro) are carefully crafted to serve and support the broader rehabilitative
structure (macro). Each lily pad interaction contributes to a larger sense of rhythm and flow.
Each visual cue serves a purpose in a bigger therapeutic arc. This coherence across scales
helps users feel oriented, supported and immersed, even in a minimalist setting with few
traditional game elements.

5.10.1 NIRVANA
BTS Nirvana is an advanced virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) rehabilitation
system designed to support motor and cognitive recovery . It provides immersive therapy ex-
periences by projecting interactive virtual environments onto surfaces, enabling patients to
engage in rehabilitation exercises in an engaging and controlled setting. The system is par-

as it facilitates motor control, balance and functional movement training (Bioengineering,
2025).
The system utilises motion-tracking technology to monitor a patient’s movements and adapt
exercises based on their performance. Patients perform exercises while responding to visual
stimuli (figure 21). Real-time feedback provides immediate performance metrics, allowing
patients and therapists to track progress. Additionally , the system supports customisable
therapy programmes, enabling therapists to adjust difficulty levels and exercise types based
on individual rehabilitation needs. The combination of visual, auditory and sometimes haptic
feedback enhances engagement and motor learning, making the rehabilitation process more
effective and motivating (Lloréns et al., 2015).

5.10.3 FitLight
FitLight is a prominent and widespread tool in rehabilitation, particularly for individuals

One of FitLight’s key applications is in neurological and cognitive rehabilitation, where it

in tasks such as tracking, hand-eye coordination and divided attention (FitLight, 2024).
In physiotherapy and musculoskeletal rehabilitation, FitLight is used to restore range of mo-

7.1 Pre-Design Research
To ensure the accuracy and precision of the exercise movements integrated into our pro-
gramme, we conducted two rounds of observational research at Gentofte Hospital. The first
round involved observing an existing STABLE programme 5.10, while the second focused
on a reactive stepping training session. Below is a table disclosing relevant detail from the
observations.
Figure 28: Observation details from STABLE programme testing and reactive stepping train-
ing
These observations provided valuable insights into both the effective components to be in-
cluded and the limitations to be addressed in the development of our own intervention.
During the observation of the STABLE programs, it became apparent that participants ex-
perienced significant challenges operating the VR controllers. Several individuals expressed
frustration during the session, specifically noting the difficulty of navigating the controllers
while simultaneously wearing the headset. This highlighted a key usability issue and in-
formed our decision to minimise reliance on hand-held controllers in our programme design,
particularly when working with older adult users. Furthermore, we observed that many
participants tended to engage with the programme in the least demanding manner possible,
avoiding more challenging movements or tasks. This behaviour suggests a tendency to priori-
tise ease over exertion, which may reduce the intended physical training effect. As a result,
we concluded that it is essential to incorporate adaptive difficulty mechanisms within the
programme in order to encourage progression and maintain an appropriate level of physical
engagement throughout the session.

The reactive stepping training session observed consisted of a structured warm-up followed
by three distinct exercises, each targeting balance, coordination, and dynamic movement in
different ways:
Exercise 1: Participants were instructed to stand in a circle and pass a ball to one another.
As the exercise progressed, additional challenges were introduced to increase complexity
and cognitive-motor engagement. These included rotational movements (spinning around),
stepping forward and backward over a rope, and performing lateral movements by walking
sideways along the rope while crossing their feet.
Exercise 2: Participants completed a multi-component obstacle course (see figure:29). The
course involved dribbling a ball through a series of cones, kicking the ball between two
planks, and completing sit-to-stand repetitions using a chair. This sequence was repeated
while balancing a feather ball on a racquet, thereby adding an additional motor coordination
challenge. While one group performed the obstacle course, the other group engaged in a
rope-based stepping activity . Here, a cluster of ropes was arranged in a circle, and partici-
pants were instructed to navigate between them using varying strategies such as minimising
or maximising the number of steps, walking on tiptoes, and moving laterally or backward
through the rope circle.
Figure 29: Illustration of exercise 2
Exercise 3: In the final exercise, participants were asked to walk among ropes of different
colours laid out on the floor (see figure:30). The physiotherapist would call out a colour,

7.4 Final design: Information, Interactive and Presentation Design
The design of a virtual reality (VR) experience is a multidisciplinary effort that integrates
multiple design principles to ensure functionality , usability , and engagement. In the context
of healthcare and rehabilitation, the success of a VR programme depends not only on its
technological capabilities but also on how effectively it communicates information, supports
user interaction, and presents its content. This chapter explores three fundamental design
types, Information Design, Interactive Design, and Presentation Design, and their roles in
shaping a user-centred VR experience.
Each design type contributes uniquely to the overall effectiveness of the system: information
design ensures that users understand what to do; interactive design determines how they
do it; and presentation design influences how they perceive and emotionally respond to the
environment. By examining these categories in the context of VR for elderly users with
vestibular dysfunction, we aim to demonstrate how thoughtful design can improve both user
experience and therapeutic outcomes.
7.4.1 Information Design
Information design in virtual reality (VR) refers to the effective structuring and presentation
of data and guidance within a 3D environment. Unlike traditional media, VR demands that
information be contextual, spatially anchored, and easily perceivable during immersive expe-
riences. This becomes particularly important in applications for healthcare, where users may
have varying levels of cognitive ability , physical limitations, or limited familiarity with digital
technologies.
97
PAGE 99
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
Figure 32: The ghost feet and frog meant to guide the user to take the first step in the
application
Upon entering the virtual programme, users are presented with a guided interface designed
to support orientation and task understanding. A set of "ghost feet" is displayed on the
virtual ground plane, serving as visual indicators of the intended foot placement (see Figure
32). These ghost feet act as preparatory cues, helping users anticipate where their next step
should be. Additionally , a virtual frog character is included as an animated guide, positioned
near or on the target stepping area. This frog functions both as a familiar and friendly visual
aid and as a spatial reference point to assist users particularly those unfamiliar with VR in
understanding task expectations.
As the user progresses through the task and crosses a predefined temporal threshold either
triggered by movement timing or dwell duration a sequence of virtual lily pads appears in the
environment. These lily pads represent step targets and are dynamically coloured to provide
real-time feedback based on the user’s path choice.
This visual feedback mechanism is designed to reinforce appropriate motor responses while
providing non-verbal, intuitive cues that inform the user of their performance. Such feedback
is particularly relevant for users undergoing vestibular rehabilitation, as it supports motor
learning, encourages adaptive stepping strategies, and minimises verbal instruction require-
ments. Additionally , the use of immediate, colour-based feedback can help physiotherapists
monitor progress and guide adjustments in real-time or post-session review.

7.4.2 Interactive Design
Interactive design in VR focuses on creating intuitive, responsive, and engaging interactions
between the user and the virtual environment. In immersive systems, interaction goes be-
yond clicking buttons; it includes physical movement, gaze, voice, and gesture-based input.
Effective interactive design ensures that users feel a sense of agency and immersion, which is
particularly valuable in therapeutic or training applications.
A central aspect of the interaction involves the user stepping onto the correct virtual lily pad,
which acts as a target zone within the environment. These targets are spatially distributed
and represent sequential stepping points, forming a structured path that the user must follow
in real time.
The user is not only expected to identify and step onto the correct lily pad but must also
progress to the subsequent target within a specified time interval. This temporal constraint
introduces a reactive component to the task, requiring users to make quick, accurate decisions
under mild cognitive and physical pressure. Such timing elements are essential in simulat-
ing real-world balance and mobility challenges, particularly for populations with vestibular
dysfunction, where delayed responses can increase fall risk.
In addition to the physical execution of steps, the user is expected to locate and recognise
the correct path from among several possibilities. This process involves the interpretation
of visual cues embedded in the environment such as spatial arrangement, directional ori-
entation, or colour variations of the lily pads which are intentionally designed to guide the
user toward the correct sequence. These perceptual and cognitive demands promote active
engagement with the environment and support the development of spatial awareness and
decision-making skills.
By integrating spatial navigation, pattern recognition, and timed movement into the task
structure, the interactive design fosters a high level of user immersion and responsiveness.
The user’s ability to correctly interpret and respond to these design elements serves as both a
therapeutic goal and an indicator of the system’s usability and effectiveness.

7.4.3 Presentation Design
Presentation design in VR concerns the visual and aesthetic aspects of how the virtual envi-
ronment and its components are experienced by the user. It includes the overall look and feel
of the environment, the spatial layout of objects and interfaces, the use of colour, lighting,
animation, and transitions. Effective presentation design supports user focus, comfort, and
emotional engagement.
From the start of the programme, the user is introduced to a nature-like environment, as
mentioned, this environment was chosen on the basis of previous STABLE programs where
a nature theme was used. The team did implement their spin on the theme while maintain-
ing the overall theme. Auditory feedback is a functional aspect of spatial orientation and
task guidance. For instance, each lily pad is accompanied by a distinct sound effect when it
emerges from the virtual water. This auditory cue aids users in rapidly identifying the loca-
tion of new stepping targets, especially in situations where visual cues may be momentarily
obscured or delayed. Additionally , the virtual frog used as a guiding character within the
environment produces a sound each time it moves from one lily pad to another. This further
supports user navigation by providing a directional audio reference that reinforces the visual
guidance offered by the frog’s movement.
In the context of our VR programme, lighting emerged as a critical aspect of presentation
design. Carefully calibrated lighting supports both immersion and usability , ensuring that
interactive elements such as stepping targets, visual cues, and avatar feedback remain clearly
visible and intuitively accessible. This is particularly important for older adults with vestibu-
lar dysfunction, who often rely on consistent visual information to maintain balance and
interpret spatial orientation.

8.3.4 Frog
At the start of the game, a frog is positioned on the start lily pad. From that point onward,
it continuously jumps from pad to pad, always remaining one step ahead of the player. The
frog was introduced to enhance several aspects of the gameplay experience. It adds a sense of
liveliness and motion to the already immersive environment, making the scene feel more dy-
namic and engaging. Visually , it serves as an appealing focal point and provides players with
a consistent target to fixate on throughout the experience. Additionally , the frog functions as
an intuitive guide, dynamically indicating the next step in the sequence. This helps reduce
player confusion by implicitly directing them toward their next move, thereby minimising the
need for explicit instructions.
Model
The frog model (Figure 52 Left) was created from scratch using Blender 4.3.2. A top-down
reference image of a frog (Figure 52 Right) was used to guide the modeling process. Starting
with a plane, the shape was extruded to match the silhouette of the frog. To add volume, the
geometry was then extruded along the Z-axis. A subdivision surface modifier was applied to
smooth the mesh and create more organic curves. For the final appearance, Blender’s shading
tools were used to apply a bright red material to the model. This colour was deliberately
chosen to ensure the frog stands out clearly against the green lily pads, making it easily
visible to the player.

9 Experiments and Results
9.1 Experiment Methods
To ensure the collection of comprehensive and meaningful test feedback, we adopted a
mixed-methods approach, integrating both qualitative and quantitative data collection tech-
niques. This approach allowed us to capture the richness of participant experiences while
also enabling structured comparisons across responses. Specifically , we employed a semi-
structured interview alongside a standardized Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ). The
semi-structured interview provided in-depth, qualitative insights into participants’ thoughts,
emotions, and subjective experiences, offering the flexibility to explore emerging themes and
follow up on unexpected responses (Kvale and Brinkmann, 2009). This method is particu-
larly well-suited for exploring complex behaviors and attitudes that may not be easily cap-
tured through closed-ended questions. In contrast, the questionnaire enabled the systematic
collection of quantifiable data, facilitating cross-participant analysis and supporting general-
izability (Creswell and Plano Clark, 2017). While structured questionnaires limit the range
of possible responses, they contribute to consistency and efficiency in data collection. By
combining these methods, we aimed to leverage the strengths of both approaches depth and
breadth to obtain a understanding of participant feedback.
9.2 Bias and Limitations
The agreement with Gentofte, as our external partner, was that they would be responsible
for recruiting and providing participants for the experiments. However, due to time con-
straints on both the research team and the hospital, the number of participants was lower
than initially anticipated. Coordinating the tests required the presence of a physiotherapist
throughout the entire testing phase, which placed additional demands on the hospital’s re-
sources and limited scheduling flexibility .
It is the project group’s intention to conduct further participant testing after the submission
of this project report. The purpose of this is to validate the current results and strengthen the
foundation for our conclusions. Any such updated findings will, if available, form the basis
for the group’s presentation and discussion of results during the final examination.
One potential bias we were particularly conscious of during the experiment was interviewer
119
PAGE 121
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
bias. In our case, this extended not only to the interviewer but also to the presence of the
note taker. Interviewer bias is a well-documented concern in qualitative and quantitative re-
search, as it can significantly influence the validity and reliability of the data collected. This
bias occurs when the interviewer, either consciously or unconsciously , affects the intervie-
wee’s responses through verbal cues, tone, question phrasing, or non-verbal behavior such as
body language or facial expressions (Opdenakker, 2006). In our study , we were particularly
attentive to the presence not only of the interviewer but also of the note taker, as both could
contribute to a heightened sense of observation. This can lead participants to modify their
responses in ways they perceive to be more socially acceptable or less personally revealing, a
phenomenon sometimes referred to as social desirability bias (Furnham, 1986). To mitigate
this, we aimed to maintain a neutral demeanour and minimize non-verbal cues during the
interview process, in line with best practices recommended in qualitative methodology (Kvale
and Brinkmann, 2009).

9.4 Experiment Results
9.4.1 Interview Results
After transcribing the interviews, we conducted a qualitative content analysis to systemati-
cally interpret the data. This method allowed us to identify patterns, themes, and meanings
embedded within the participants’ responses (Mayring, 2014). The transcriptions were coded
using a combination of deductive and inductive coding approaches. Initially , we applied a set
of predefined categories, developed based on our research questions and theoretical frame-
work, to guide the analysis. At the same time, we remained open to the emergence of new,
data-driven categories, which were identified during the coding process and incorporated as
needed to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the material. The predefined categories
used in the analysis included:
• Usability - the overall use of the program and hardware
• User Experience - the overall user experience of the program
• Cybersickness - Whether or not the participants experienced any discomfort during or
after the game
• AR/VR - Their preference or any comment to the overall VR or AR experience
Categories found during coding:
• Elderly’s Acceptance- whether or not the participants were open and accepting of the
program and equipment.
• Familiarity/Relevance - the relevance of the game and how it relates to their training
By applying these categories, we aimed to capture both anticipated and unanticipated di-
mensions of participant feedback, allowing for a nuanced interpretation that reflects both
theoretical expectations and user-driven insights.
To enhance the reliability and validity of our qualitative content analysis, we employed a dual

coding process, wherein two independent researchers coded the same set of interview tran-
scripts. Dual coding helps ensure that the interpretation of data is not overly influenced by a

codes that emerged from the data. Following the initial round of coding, the researchers com-

analysis but also helps to surface alternative interpretations, ultimately enriching the depth
and credibility of the findings.

9.4.3 Cohen’s Kappa
Cohen’s Kappa is a mean to measure the inter-rater reliability . In other terms, it measures the
agreement between two dependant raters’ categorical data, this is used to figure out if the
rater’s transcript coding is in agreement.
Cohen’s Kappa is designed for nominal data. Nominal variables are categorical variables with
no order, for example gender, diagnosis, connotation, etc.. Whereas, ordinal variables are
categorical variables with an order, but unequal spacing between them, for example pain
scale, and metric variables are numerical variables where spacing is meaningful, for example
time taken to complete tasks. (DATAtab, 2025)
The reasoning behind using Cohen’s Kappa is to determine the reliability between the two
raters’ measurements’ similarity (the inter-rater reliability , not to be confused with intra-
reliability which is the agreement between different measurements made by the same per-
son). Meaning; are they reliably measuring the same thing? Cohen’s Kappa does not de-
termine the validity of their measurements, therefore we cannot conclude if what they are
measuring is correct or not, on this basis. It is only used to assess the consistency between
two raters or coders.
Since we have nominal data (the connotation) and want to test the agreement between the
raters’ measurements, we will use Cohen’s Kappa. To do so the first step is to create a table
that contains the frequency of the positive and negative rating combinations between the
raters. This is done by keeping a tally of how many times each combinations occurs (both
code positive, both code negative, rater 1 codes positive whilst rater 2 codes negative, and
rater 1 codes negative whilst rater 2 codes positive) and putting those values under the
corresponding area (DATAtab, 2025). The table containing our values can be seen in Figure
59.

As mentioned in 9.3.2 a cybersickness test was performed three times during each individual
test. A preliminary test to establish a baseline score, one after the first playthrough, and one
after the final playthrough. This approach allowed us to monitor each participant’s level of
cybersickness throughout the experiment and detect any notable changes over time.
The questionnaire used was a standard 4-point Likert-scale version of the SSQ A.2.1 heavily

measured were indicators of discomfort (i.e. nausea, sweat tendency , headaches etc.).
9.5.1 Results and Discussion of Cybersickness Questionnaire
The results of the cybersickness questionnaire tests mostly backs up what was discovered
during the post-test interviews. approximately two thirds of the 60 questions (5 participants,
12 questions each) recorded the same score in all 3 tests, meaning there were few changes
in most cybersickness-indicating symptoms from start to finish of the experiment.
In the remaining questions, which recorded variances in scores, there are very few discernable
patterns. For example, several participants scored some symptoms higher in the baseline test
than in the second or third test. It is hard to attribute this to a particular phenomenon. If
the validity of the results was blindly trusted, it could be deduced that the game reduces
cybersickness. Although studies suggest that an experience inducing high levels of presence
can reduce the users’ perceived level of cybersickness (Weech et al., 2019), it is unlikely that
this particular trend in test scores can be attributed to this phenomenon.
A more plausible explanation for the anomalies in the results, is the fact that we relied solely
on self-reports for this section of the experiment. Here, the use of physiological sensors could
have been used to obtain more precise results, but for some of the same reasons as discussed
in 5.5.3 we opted to rely on other ways to extract information.
Moreover, cybersickness was also addressed during the interview phase, providing a comple-
mentary qualitative perspective. It was agreed among the research team that the combina-
tion of structured questionnaire data and observational/interview-based insights would be
sufficient to identify meaningful cybersickness trends. We also reasoned that if a participant
experienced significant discomfort, it would be apparent to the observer or emerge during
the interview.
However, as mentioned in 9.4.2 we have to acknowledge the limitations regarding the tim-
ing of symptom emergence. Some participants reported, off the record, that symptoms such
as dizziness appeared only after the testing session had ended. At that point, it was too late to
administer an additional SSQ. Nevertheless, this observation is a valuable lesson and should
inform the design of future experiments. Administering a follow-up SSQ after a short delay

post-experience could yield more comprehensive insights.
In conclusion, the results suggest that most users tolerated the experience well, at least in
the immediate aftermath. The generally low variation in SSQ scores, supported by inter-
view data, indicates that the measures taken to mitigate cybersickness, including research
informed design choices and preliminary testing, were largely successful.
9.6 Expert Feedback
To complement user-based evaluations of the VR and AR training program, we conducted
qualitative unstructured interviews and observational reviews with field experts. These ex-
perts provided critical and constructive feedback on the programme’s design, engagement
potential, and relevance in both individual and group-based training contexts. Their insights
were instrumental in identifying opportunities for improvement, as well as in affirming key
strengths of the system.
Experts noted several strengths of the VR-based training approach, particularly its capacity
to engage users in multi-sensory and balance-related tasks. One expert highlighted that the
VR environment effectively challenges users’ vestibular systems and balance control, which
are essential areas of focus in geriatric training.
The visual immersion offered by VR was also seen as a valuable component. Experts remarked
that, unlike standard physical training, VR allows for greater manipulation of visual stimuli to
enhance perceptual training. For example, it was suggested that visual distractions, such as
animated fish in the water or moving focal points like a frog, could be introduced to further
stimulate visual processing and selective attention in a controlled yet dynamic manner. This
led one expert to propose the inclusion of a new training domain, referred to as the "visual
domain," dedicated to visual-perceptual challenges in motor tasks.
While the VR programme was generally well-received, several design limitations were noted.
A key concern was the lack of variation in stepping patterns, particularly on the easier route.
Experts observed that steps remained overly uniform, which reduced both physical engage-
ment and cognitive stimulation. It was recommended that the programme vary step distances
incorporating both longer and shorter steps to better simulate real-world walking patterns
and more effectively engage different motor domains.
137
PAGE 139
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
Additionally , the transition mechanics between elements, such as lily pads, were critiqued.
Experts noted that these objects sometimes sank too suddenly and without sufficient warn-
ing, which could lead to confusion or discomfort, especially for users with slower reaction
times. One expert suggested incorporating motivational incentives such as time-based pro-
gression rewards to encourage users to move efficiently through the task and maintain mo-
mentum.
Another practical insight concerned the context in which the technology would be used. Ex-
perts agreed that while VR is more suitable for structured group training sessions, AR would
likely be more effective for home-based exercises, due to its lower spatial and sensory de-
mands. This distinction supports a context-sensitive deployment strategy , where VR is re-
served for immersive, therapist-guided sessions, and AR supplements continued engagement
at home.
9.6.1 Summary: Recommendations and Future Directions
Based on expert feedback, the following enhancements are recommended:
• Introduce variability in task demands, including variable step lengths and movement
complexity .
• Add preparatory visual or auditory cues before interactive elements change (e.g., lily
pads sinking).
• Incorporate visual distractors and focal points to develop a “visual domain” for perceptual-
motor integration.
• Develop adaptive difficulty settings to adjust challenge levels based on user perfor-
mance.
• Differentiate use cases for VR and AR, aligning VR with supervised training and AR with
independent use.

Throughout this project, we employed the Spiral Model as our primary development method-

aligned well with the unpredictable nature of working with elderly users and hardware-based
solutions. Other development models were also reviewed such as the waterfall and V-model
but were ultimately eliminated mostly due to their more rigorous and structured format.

nate development phases and distribute tasks effectively . Task division often followed indi-
vidual strengths, reflecting a loose adaptation of Belbin’s Team Roles (Belbin, 2010). This

contributing to cross-cutting activities like testing and documentation.
However, the real-world execution was not without its challenges. As with many student
projects, progress was non-linear, sections were restructured multiple times, and planning
often had to adjust around shifting priorities, availability of test participants, and newly
emerging technical hurdles. Despite this, we consistently returned to our roadmap to re-
orient ourselves and maintain momentum.

10.3 Evaluation Results
10.3.1 Experiment Setup
The experiment setup was carefully designed to ensure both safety and meaningful data col-
lection, particularly given the elderly target group. One of the main priorities was to simulate
realistic conditions, which is why the final evaluation took place with support from physio-
therapists. The use of both qualitative (interviews and expert feedback) and quantitative
(questionnaires) methods allowed for a more well-rounded understanding of the user expe-
rience.
Choosing to randomise the order of AR and VR exposure helped minimise order effects and
ensured a more balanced comparison between modalities. it should be noted that the or-
der in which the modalities were tested could potentially affect the results. For example,
the participants who tested AR first, could possibly be more comfortable when testing VR,
having already become accustomed to wearing the headset while still being able to see their
surroundings. Similarly , administering cybersickness questionnaires before and after each
experience allowed us to assess immediate physiological responses in a structured manner.
However, while the setup succeeded in capturing a variety of insights, the complexity of coor-
dinating multiple roles (facilitators, note taker, physiotherapist, technical support) meant that
some environmental consistency across sessions may have varied. The presence of multiple
people in the room may also have affected participant behaviour. While this setup ensured
safety and operational support, it could also have introduced social pressure or distracted
participants, potentially influencing their sense of presence, perceived performance, or will-
ingness to express discomfort. The number of participants was also limited, partly due to
141
PAGE 143
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
hospital scheduling constraints. These factors should be acknowledged when interpreting
the generalisability of the results.
The structure of the setup reflected a balance between methodological rigour and practical
limitations. These choices enabled us to prioritise participant comfort and data richness, even
within the constraints of a real-world clinical testing environment.
10.3.2 Interview Results
The interview results were generally very positive, with participants expressing enjoyment,
curiosity , and a willingness to use the system again. However, when reflecting on these
responses, it is important to consider the context in which they were given. For most par-
ticipants, this was their first time trying VR, and their limited prior exposure may have in-
fluenced their ability to critically assess the system. With no baseline for comparison, even
simple interactions or visual feedback may have appeared surprisingly advanced or impres-
sive. As mentioned, the presence of multiple observers and the novelty of the experience
could also have contributed to overly positive feedback, possibly influenced by social desir-
ability bias. This was supported by our observational notes, which indicated moments of
imbalance or hesitation during the exercises, issues that were rarely mentioned in the inter-
views. These discrepancies suggest that while interviews offered valuable insights into user
perceptions and attitudes, they may not fully reflect the challenges participants encountered
in practice. Future studies should consider combining interviews with more structured be-
havioural metrics or follow-up sessions to better capture long-term impressions and usability
concerns.
10.3.3 Cohen’s Kappa Results
The Cohen’s Kappa value of 0.922 indicates an almost perfect level of agreement between
the two raters. While this result is encouraging, it should be interpreted with caution. The
high score may reflect clear and unambiguous behaviour in the material, but it could also
be influenced by factors that limit its generalisability . The rating task was relatively simple
and binary , which naturally reduces the chance of disagreement. Additionally , most ratings
were positive, creating a skewed distribution that can inflate the agreement and the resulting
Kappa value. It’s also possible that the raters shared prior experience or expectations, espe-
cially given their involvement in the development process, which could have unintentionally
142
PAGE 144
Project Report - 6th semester - Group 604 Medialogy - Aalborg University - Copenhagen
aligned their judgments. Lastly , the binary rating scale offers little room for nuance, which
may exaggerate the perceived consistency .
The result suggests the rating task was clear in this context, but future studies with more
complex behaviours, diverse data, or a multi-level coding scheme would give a more reliable
picture of inter-rater agreement.
10.3.4 SSQ Results
The cybersickness questionnaire results, supported by interview and observational data, sug-
gest that most participants tolerated the VR experience well. Symptoms remained largely
stable across sessions, with minimal increases in discomfort. However, interpretation is com-
plicated by the reliance on self-reports, which may not capture delayed onset symptoms.
Notably , some participants mentioned off-record that dizziness occurred only after the ses-
sion ended, highlighting the value of including a follow-up questionnaire in future studies,
especially since it remains unclear whether the dizziness was caused by the test itself or
their underlying vestibular dysfunction. A further complexity distinguishing between cyber-
sickness and therapeutically induces dizziness. As noted by a physiotherapist we consulted,
inducing mild dizziness is often intentional in vestibular rehabilitation, helping patients train
their balance responses. In this context, a certain level of dizziness can be positive, unlike
cybersickness, which is disruptive and undesirable. The two are difficult to differentiate,
without proper professional background knowledge, especially for first-time VR users, who
may lack the experience to report symptoms clearly or critically .
Overall, while the SSQ data suggests our measures to minimise cybersickness were largely
successful, future evaluations would benefit from delayed symptom tracking, objective mea-
sures and clearer frameworks to distinguish discomfort types. This would allow for a more
precise understanding of user experience and system safety .

predefined themes, recording and post-interview analysis, would support more robust docu-
mentation, that would be easy to trace. Nonetheless, the inclusion of informal expert input
added a critical layer of external validation to our development proceed and highlighted de-
sign opportunities we may not have uncovered through the user testing alone. It also helped
us validate our proof of concept.

11 Conclusion
This project set out to investigate and compare the potential of Virtual Reality and Augmented
Reality in supporting elderly individuals with vestibular dysfunction during reactive stepping
rehabilitation exercises. The aim was to assess which modality offers greater feasibility , us-
ability , and therapeutic effectiveness in alignment with the STABLE framework, focusing on
the reactive stepping domain.
Through extensive research, iterative design, and a user-centred approach, we developed
a Mixed Reality-based rehabilitation system using Meta Quest 3 hardware. Employing the
Spiral Model enabled a flexible and adaptive development cycle, allowing for continuous
integration of user feedback, expert consultation, and technological refinement. The final
system featured both VR and AR implementations, providing a robust comparative testing
and evaluation platform.
Usability studies, cybersickness assessments, and qualitative interviews with both elderly par-
ticipants and physiotherapists revealed several key findings. VR was found to be highly im-
mersive and effective in promoting focused engagement and bodily awareness attributes cru-
cial for vestibular training. However, some participants reported initial hesitation or slight
discomfort due to the enclosed nature of VR headsets. Conversely , AR was generally perceived
as more intuitive and less disorienting, particularly for first-time users and those with greater
concerns about spatial orientation and safety . Nevertheless, the lower level of immersion in
AR occasionally limited its ability to guide complex or full-body movements. Cybersickness
levels remained low across both modalities, suggesting that careful design and optimisation,
including visual clarity , movement pacing, and user interface simplicity , can substantially
minimise such risks, even in sensitive populations. Physiotherapist feedback further con-
firmed that the exercises were well aligned with clinical practices and provided a valuable
supplement to traditional therapy methods.
The project also reinforced the importance of multimodal feedback, adaptive task complex-
ity , and environmental familiarity in designing XR-based rehabilitation tools for elderly users.
The integration of real-time feedback, gamified elements, and personalised training path-
ways contributed to increased motivation, confidence, and task adherence among partici-
pants.

In conclusion, both VR and AR demonstrate strong potential in augmenting rehabilitation
practices for vestibular dysfunction. While VR offers deeper engagement and more immersive
environments for motor training, AR provides a gentler learning curve and may be better
suited for initial onboarding or home-based use. The choice between the two should be
informed by context-specific needs, user preferences, and clinical goals. This study not only
contributes to the growing body of knowledge in XR healthcare applications but also lays
the foundation for future research and development in creating inclusive, accessible, and
effective digital rehabilitation tools.

12 Future Works
There are several areas where the system could be further developed to improve its function-
ality , usability , and impact on rehabilitation outcomes. Improving the stability and accuracy
of foot-tracking remains a priority , and exploring custom hardware solutions might offer a
path forward. Expanding the participant pool to include a wider range of older adults, espe-
cially those with different types of vestibular challenges, would also provide valuable insights
and improve the system’s generalizability .
Personalization is another important goal designing adaptive difficulty features that adjust to
each user’s performance in real-time could make rehabilitation more effective and engaging.
Additionally , future work could compare VR and AR interventions more comprehensively by
including physiological measures like heart rate variability and muscle activation, offering a
deeper understanding of how each approach supports balance training.
Long-term testing is crucial for understanding how the system performs in real-world set-
tings, including sustained user engagement, improvements in balance, and adherence over
extended periods. Finally , integrating simple data visualization tools would help physiother-
apists monitor user progress, identify trends, and tailor interventions more effectively .
In summary , future work could focus on:
• Improving foot-tracking accuracy .
• Expanding participant diversity .
• Implementing adaptive difficulty .
• Deepening VR and AR comparisons.
• Conducting long-term evaluations.
• Building tools for progress tracking.

5. Test Setup
• Equipment: Meta Quest 3 headset and controllers, cybersickness questionnaire, inter-

• Environment: Quiet, comfortable setting with minimal distractions
• Monitoring: Real-time observation + post-task interviews and questionnaires
6. Test Procedure Upon arrival, the patient is escorted into the designated testing room,
where they are directed to the appropriate position for the experiment. This position is care-
fully selected to optimise the participant’s engagement with the immersive system, ensuring
that they are comfortably situated within the intended field of view and interaction range of
the virtual or augmented reality setup. The setup is adjusted to accommodate any specific
physical or environmental considerations, such as seating arrangements or proximity to the
equipment. The research team ensures that the participant is at ease and properly oriented
within the space before proceeding with further instructions or the commencement of the
testing session.
1. Introduction
• At the outset of the session, the test facilitators introduce themselves and clearly
explain the purpose of the evaluation to the participant, ensuring that the patient
understands the context and goals of the testing procedure.
• Following this, the patient is guided through the completion of the pre-test cyber-
sickness questionnaire, which assesses baseline symptoms such as nausea, visual
discomfort, and dizziness. Assistance is provided by a member of the project team
or the attending physiotherapist, as needed, to ensure accurate comprehension
and response. Participants are explicitly encouraged to ask questions or seek clari-

fication if they encounter any confusion while filling out the questionnaire.
• Once the questionnaire is completed, the patient is introduced to the virtual reality
(VR) system. This includes a demonstration of the head-mounted display (HMD)
and handheld controllers, with a clear explanation of their functions and usage.
The equipment is then carefully fitted onto the patient, with particular attention
paid to comfort, stability , and safety . The team ensures that the headset and con-
trollers are properly adjusted to suit the individual, minimising physical discomfort
and optimising the user experience.
2. Task Completion (part 1)
• Following the initial setup, the patient proceeds to complete the first interactive
task, which consists of a randomised virtual or augmented reality game. This
randomised assignment ensures variation in exposure and helps mitigate potential
order effects in the evaluation of user experience and cybersickness symptoms.
• Throughout the task, the patient is informed that they may ask questions or request
clarification at any time. Assistance from the research team is permitted as needed,
though any interventions are recorded to maintain transparency in the usability
evaluation process. This approach aims to balance user autonomy with ethical
considerations for safety and comfort, particularly in cases where unfamiliarity
with VR/AR technology may affect task performance.
• An assigned observer closely monitors the session, documenting any usability is-
sues such as navigation difficulties, interaction errors, or confusion with system
feedback. Additionally , signs of physical discomfort—including facial tension, shifts
in posture, or verbal expressions of unease—are noted. These observations con-
tribute to the qualitative assessment of the prototype’s usability and its potential
to induce cybersickness-related symptoms.
3. Cybersickness Questionnaire (e.g., Simulator Sickness Questionnaire – SSQ)
• Immediately upon completion of the VR/AR task, the patient is asked to complete
a second cybersickness questionnaire, identical in structure to the pre-test version.
This post-exposure assessment is designed to capture any changes in the partici-
pant’s physical state resulting from interaction with the immersive environment.

Administering the questionnaire directly after task completion ensures the accu-
racy and immediacy of the self-reported symptoms. Participants are reminded that
they may ask questions or seek clarification if any items are unclear. Addition-
ally , they are offered the option to sit down during this phase, to accommodate
potential fatigue, dizziness, or general discomfort, common effects in VR and AR
environments, particularly among novice users.
• The questionnaire captures data on several key symptom clusters, including nau-
sea, oculomotor strain (e.g., eye fatigue, difficulty focusing), and disorientation
(e.g., dizziness, vertigo). These symptom categories are then scored to assess the
severity and frequency of cybersickness symptoms. Comparing pre- and post-test
scores enables an objective evaluation of the prototype’s physical impact on the
user.
1. Task Completion (part 2)
• After completing the post-exposure questionnaire, the patient proceeds to engage
with the second interactive task, which is the opposite modality of the first game
(i.e., if the first game was in virtual reality , the second is in augmented reality ,
and vice versa). This counterbalancing approach is used to compare the effects of
different immersive technologies on user experience and cybersickness symptoms
while controlling for individual variability . As with the previous task, the patient
is informed that they may ask questions at any time during the interaction. Sup-
port from the research team is available if required, ensuring that participants feel
comfortable and safe while engaging with the system. However, any assistance
provided is documented for transparency and to inform the usability analysis.
• An observer continues to monitor the session closely , recording any usability chal-
lenges, interaction errors, or moments of confusion, as well as signs of physical
discomfort. These observations are essential for identifying potential design im-
provements and for understanding how different immersive environments may
impact user experience and well-being.

2. Post-Test Interview
• Following the completion of the final cybersickness questionnaire, a semi-structured
interview is conducted to gather in-depth qualitative feedback from the patient.
This interview format allows for a consistent set of core questions while also pro-
viding flexibility to explore relevant topics that emerge during the conversation.
• The patient is informed that they may ask for clarification at any point if any
questions or terminology are unclear. This ensures that the interview remains
participant-friendly and that the responses accurately reflect the individual’s expe-
rience.
• The interview is designed to explore several key themes, including perceived us-
ability of the system, physical and cognitive comfort during use, and any difficulties
encountered while interacting with the VR and AR environments. Participants are
also encouraged to provide open-ended suggestions for improvement, allowing for
the identification of user-driven priorities and potential design enhancements. The
data gathered through this interview complements the observational and quantita-

1. Debrief
• To conclude the session, the facilitator provides the participant with a brief sum-
mary of the next steps in the research process. This may include how the collected
data will be analyzed, how the findings will contribute to the ongoing develop-
ment of the system, and whether there will be any follow-up or opportunities for
future participation.
• Participants are also informed of how their data will be stored and handled in
accordance with ethical guidelines, emphasising confidentiality and the voluntary
nature of their involvement.
• Finally , the research team sincerely thanks the participant for their time, cooper-
ation, and valuable input. Their contribution is acknowledged as essential to the
refinement of the system and the success of the project overall.

7. Metrics and Data Collection
• Cybersickness Scores: From questionnaire (quantitative)
• Task Observations: Errors, delays, hesitations (qualitative & quantitative)
• Interview Insights: cybersickness, usability , preferences
• Engagement or Comfort Ratings: Optional scale (e.g., 1–5)
8. Sample Post-Test Interview Questions
• How did you feel physically during the session? Any discomfort or dizziness? (0-3)
• Were any parts of the interface confusing or difficult to use?
• What would you change or improve?
• Would you feel comfortable using this regularly?
